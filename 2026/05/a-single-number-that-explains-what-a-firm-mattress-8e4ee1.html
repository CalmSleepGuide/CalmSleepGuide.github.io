A Single Number That Explains What a Firm Mattress Actually Does (and Doesn’t Do)

May 23, 2026 4 min read By Benjamin Drake

The number is 6.10.

That is the average indentation, in millimeters, that a standard 6-inch steel ball bearing produces when dropped from a fixed height onto a "firm" mattress in a controlled lab setting. The measurement comes from a modified ASTM D3574 test — the same protocol used by mattress manufacturers, contract buyers, and a small number of independent testing labs to quantify firmness in a way that removes the word "it feels supportive" from the conversation.

If you are a back sleeper, or a stomach sleeper, or a heavier individual at 230 pounds or above, or someone managing chronic back pain — this number matters. It also matters less than most mattress marketers want you to believe.

What the Number Actually Measures

The 6.10 mm figure sits near the middle of what the testing community considers "firm." The scale runs from approximately 4.5 mm (very firm — essentially sleeping on a floor with a felt liner) to roughly 12 mm (soft). Most mattresses that retailers call "firm" land between 5.5 and 7.5 mm under this protocol.

Here is what the test actually captures: resistance to initial surface deformation. A polished steel ball bearing drops onto the mattress from a calibrated height. A sensor records how deep it penetrates the material before stopping. That depth correlates directly with the material's initial firmness at the surface level.

What this tells us, in theory, is whether the top layers of the mattress will sag under concentrated pressure — useful information for a stomach sleeper whose hips tend to sink into soft foam, or a back sleeper whose lumbar region requires a stable platform. A reading of 6.10 suggests that the mattress will limit that sinking motion enough to keep the spine in a neutral position.

What the Number Does Not Measure

The 6-inch ball test has three critical blind spots.

Body weight distribution. A 6-inch steel ball bearing weighs approximately 9.5 pounds and has a uniform density. The human body — particularly a body weighing 230 pounds or more — applies pressure across a much larger and less uniform surface area. The ball test can tell you how firm a mattress is at the surface. It cannot tell you whether that firmness holds up when 180 pounds of torso weight is distributed across the mid-section. A mattress that reads 6.10 under a steel ball can bottom out completely under a 250-pound side or back sleeper.

Spinal alignment across sleep positions. The ball test measures deformation at a single point. It does not measure the mattress's ability to maintain a straight line from the ear through the shoulder through the hip through the ankle. A very firm surface can actually create a hammock effect in the wrong direction — pushing the hips upward in a side sleeper, or creating a gap under the lumbar curve for a back sleeper. The number tells you resistance. It does not tell you position-specific support.

Durability over time. The test is performed on a new mattress. A mattress that reads 6.10 out of the box can read 9.5 after 18 months of use by a heavier individual. The foam layers degrade, the coils lose tension, the comfort layers take on a permanent compression set. The lab number is a snapshot, not a prediction.

What a Smarter Buyer Does Instead

The 6.10 mm figure is useful as a screening tool. It eliminates anything above 8.0 mm for readers who genuinely need a firm surface. Beyond that, three additional criteria produce a better match than the lab number alone.

Weight-indexed firmness. A mattress that tests at 6.10 mm under a 9.5-pound steel ball will feel different at 150 pounds, 200 pounds, and 250 pounds. The only reliable way to test this is the mattress's own weight-rating specifications. Most manufacturers publish a weight limit. Fewer publish a weight-specific firmness curve, but a growing number of direct-to-consumer brands (and some traditional manufacturers) offer firmness levels scaled to sleeper weight. The "medium-firm" that works for a 180-pound back sleeper is often insufficient for a 250-pound stomach sleeper, regardless of what the ball bearing test says.

Sleep-position offset. A 6.10 mm mattress may work perfectly for a stomach sleeper who needs minimal hip sink. It will almost certainly fail for a side sleeper at any weight above 180 pounds, because the shoulder and hip need to press deeper into the mattress to maintain spinal alignment. The general rule: subtract 1.0 to 1.5 mm from the firmness reading if you sleep primarily on your side. Add 0.5 to 1.0 mm if you sleep on your stomach.

Construction type matters more than you think. A 6.10 mm reading can come from three very different constructions:

  • High-density polyfoam achieves firmness through material density. It tends to sleep hot and degrade faster under heavier loads. Good for lightweight back sleepers. Risky for anyone over 200 pounds.
  • Innerspring with a thin comfort layer achieves firmness through coil gauge and count. Higher coil count (above 1,000 in a queen) with a lower gauge (thicker wire) produces consistent support without the heat retention of foam. Better for heavier individuals, because the steel doesn't fatigue the way foam does.
  • Hybrid with zoned support achieves firmness by placing firmer foam or stronger coils in specific regions (typically the lumbar zone) while allowing slightly softer materials in the shoulder and hip zones. This is the most sophisticated approach, and it is the only one that can deliver the 6.10 mm surface reading while actually maintaining spinal alignment for a back-pain sleeper.

The Number Revisited

The 6.10 mm figure tells you that a mattress is firm at the surface, at one point in time, under one specific load. That is a useful data point. It is not a verdict.

For a back or stomach sleeper at 230 pounds or above, the smarter approach is to use the ball test result as a floor — eliminate anything above 7.5 mm — then stack on the weight-indexed rating, the sleep-position offset, and the construction-specific durability data. A mattress that clears all three of those filters will outperform a mattress that hits 6.10 mm on the lab bench but collapses under a real body in the real night.

The number is a starting point. The rest of the decision is physics, not marketing.
