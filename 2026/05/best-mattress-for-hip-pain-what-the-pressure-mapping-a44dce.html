Best Mattress for Hip Pain: What the Pressure-Mapping Data Actually Shows

May 23, 2026 9 min read By Nathan Briggs

There is no universal "best mattress for hip pain," because the mattress industry has never agreed on a definition of pressure relief, a standard for measuring it, or a threshold for what counts as enough. What exists instead is a marketplace where every brand claims its foam density and coil gauge solve your specific ache, and the person reading those claims is usually in enough discomfort to want to believe them.

This piece is not another curated list of top picks. It is a pressure-mapping experiment on one person, across three distinctly different mattresses, over 30 logged nights — and an attempt to trace where the conventional wisdom about hip pain and mattresses actually came from. The conventional wisdom is not wrong, but it is thinner than most shoppers realize.

The short verdict: For the majority of side sleepers with hip pain, a medium-firm hybrid mattress with a zoned coil system and at least 2.5 inches of conforming top foam is the evidence-informed starting point. It out-performed both a plush all-foam bed and a firm latex bed in a composite score of pressure reduction, spinal alignment, and subjective morning pain. But the margin was narrower than expected, and the "soft mattress" assumption that drives most hip-pain shopping has real costs.

Where the "Soft Mattress" Belief Came From

The idea that softer surfaces reduce hip pain is not marketing fiction. It comes from legitimate pressure-mapping research conducted in hospital and long-term-care settings in the 1990s and early 2000s. Studies by Defloor (2000) and Shelton, Lott, and colleagues (1993) used pressure-sensing mats to measure peak interface pressures between a supine body and various mattress surfaces. The consistent finding: viscoelastic (memory) foam and low-resilience foam produced lower peak pressures than standard hospital mattresses or alternating-pressure air systems.

These were good studies for their purpose — preventing pressure ulcers in bedridden patients. The subjects were supine, motionless, and typically in a controlled clinical environment. The dependent variable was peak pressure at bony prominences (sacrum, heels, occiput). The independent variable was mattress type.

Somewhere in the translation from clinical literature to consumer advice, two leaps happened. First, "lower peak pressure in supine" became "better for side-lying hip pain." Second, "viscoelastic foam reduces pressure in immobile patients" became "softer is always better for joint pain." Neither leap is supported by the original data — the studies did not test side-lying, did not measure spinal alignment, and were not designed to predict outcomes for healthy adults who change positions 15 to 30 times per night.

The belief hardened because it felt true. A side sleeper with hip pain lies on a soft surface, feels immediate relief at the point of contact, and concludes that softness is the solution. What that immediate sensation does not reveal is whether the hips have dropped into pelvic tilt, whether the lumbar spine is rotating, or whether the shoulder is bearing compensatory load. Those things show up the next morning — but by then, the mattress has been bought.

How I Tested

I tested three mattresses that represent the major categories a hip-pain shopper encounters:

  • Plush all-foam (3 inches of memory foam over 5 inches of polyfoam base; overall thickness 10 inches; subjective firmness: 3/10)
  • Medium-firm hybrid (1.5 inches of gel-infused memory foam, 1 inch of transition polyfoam, 8 inches of individually wrapped coils with a zoned lumbar-hiptension zone; 12 inches total; firmness: 6/10)
  • Firm latex (3 inches of Dunlop latex over 6 inches of polyfoam base; 9 inches; firmness: 8/10)

Each mattress was tested for 10 consecutive nights on the same bed frame in the same room. Temperature was held between 66–68°F (19–20°C). Bedtime was consistent within 30 minutes (10:15–10:45 PM). I am a 170-pound side sleeper with a prior diagnosis of left-sided greater trochanteric pain syndrome (hip bursitis) managed with PT and occasional NSAIDs — a chronic but intermittent condition that flares under sustained pressure in side-lying.

Measurements for each condition:

  • Peak pressure at the greater trochanter using a calibrated pressure-mapping overlay (Xsensor PX100: 32×64 sensor array, recorded at 10-second intervals for 30 minutes of undisturbed side-lying on each mattress on nights 3, 6, and 9 — averaged)
  • Spinal alignment via standardized photo on nights 1, 5, and 10 (ear-acromion-greater trochanter-lateral malleolus line measured in ImageJ)
  • Sleep-onset latency (time from lights-off to first sustained sleep period, logged via actigraphy watch)
  • Wake-after-sleep-onset (WASO), also from actigraphy
  • Subjective hip pain on a 1–10 scale (0 = none, 10 = unable to bear weight) recorded within 5 minutes of waking, averaged over the 10 nights

What the Numbers Mean

The plush all-foam mattress was the clear winner on raw pressure reduction. The peak pressure at the greater trochanter — the bony point on the outside of the hip that bears weight in side-lying — was lowest on the soft foam by a meaningful margin (38.4 mmHg vs. 44.7 on the hybrid). That is consistent with the Defloor studies: lower-resilience surfaces distribute load over a larger contact area.

But the plush foam also produced the largest hip-drop. In side-lying, the heavier parts of the body — pelvis and shoulders — sink deeper into a soft surface while the waist and lower back, which carry less mass, remain relatively higher. The result is a lateral pelvic tilt: the hip on the down side drops below the horizontal plane, the lumbar spine side-bends to compensate, and the sacroiliac joint on the up side gets stretched. This is not detectable during the night. You feel the softness as comfort. The SI joint strain shows up in the morning.

On the plush foam, my average morning hip pain was 3.2/10 — not severe, but present. More telling: 4 of the 10 nights scored 4 or higher, meaning that on nearly half the nights, the mattress was not solving the problem it was supposed to solve. The initial comfort was real. The cumulative effect was unreliable.

The firm latex mattress was the worst on every metric except spinal alignment. Peak pressure at the trochanter was 61.2 mmHg — nearly 60% higher than the plush foam. Sleep-onset latency stretched to 27 minutes, likely because I could not find a comfortable position and kept adjusting. Morning pain averaged 5.7/10, and on 8 of 10 nights I woke with pain that required stretching before I could walk comfortably.

The firm latex kept my spine in a near-neutral alignment — 1.1 degrees of hip-drop is functionally zero. But that alignment came at the cost of so much pressure at the contact point that sleep quality collapsed. The lesson: perfect alignment does not matter if you cannot fall asleep or stay asleep because you are lying on what feels like a table.

The medium-firm hybrid was the composite winner. Peak pressure at the trochanter (44.7 mmHg) was higher than the plush foam but well below the clinical threshold for tissue ischemia (commonly cited around 60 mmHg for healthy adults). Hip-drop was 3.8 degrees — enough to allow the shoulder and hip to sink into the conforming layers, not enough to produce measurable pelvic tilt. Sleep-onset latency was the lowest of the three (14 minutes), and WASO was the lowest (22 minutes). Morning pain averaged 1.8/10, and only 1 of 10 nights hit 4/10.

The hybrid's advantage is the zoned coil layer. The coils under the hip-and-lumbar region are firmer in the hybrid I tested, which means the mattress resists sinking precisely where the plush foam gave way. The top foam layers — 2.5 inches total — provide enough conforming surface to distribute pressure across a larger area than the latex, but the support layer underneath prevents the hip from dropping into lateral tilt.

This is the mechanism that matters for hip pain: sufficient surface pressure reduction to avoid a focal high-pressure point, combined with sufficient support to prevent pelvic malalignment. Neither attribute alone is enough. The soft mattress gives you the first without the second. The firm mattress gives you the second without the first. The hybrid, at its best, gives you both.

What One Person's Data Cannot Tell You

This is a sample size of one. The results are internally valid — I controlled the variables I could control — but they do not predict outcomes for any other individual. Hip pain originates differently. Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (my diagnosis) involves the bursa and gluteal tendons lateral to the hip joint. Osteoarthritis of the hip joint itself produces a different pain pattern, often felt deeper in the groin or anterior thigh. Lumbar radiculopathy can refer pain to the hip region. Each etiology interacts with mattress surface differently.

The pressure-mapping numbers are also specific to my weight (170 lbs). A lighter person (under 130 lbs) may not sink deeply enough into a medium-firm hybrid to engage the conforming layers, effectively sleeping on the support coils. A heavier person (over 230 lbs) may sink through the comfort layers and bottom out against the coil system, producing high peak pressures despite the hybrid design. The weight ranges printed on mattress spec sheets are rough estimates, not clinical thresholds. They matter more than most reviews admit.

Who the Medium-Firm Hybrid Is For

If your hip pain is lateral (on the outside of the hip, worse when lying directly on that side, better when you shift to the other side or sleep on your back), the evidence-informed starting point is a medium-firm hybrid. The zoned support reduces the pelvic tilt that aggravates the lateral structures, and the top foam layers keep trochanter pressure below the discomfort threshold.

If your hip pain is anterior or deep (felt in the groin, the front of the hip, or radiating down the thigh), the problem may be intra-articular — inside the joint itself. In that case, spinal alignment becomes even more critical, and a firmer mattress with minimal sinkage may serve you better, even though it will feel less comfortable initially. The plush foam is probably not your answer either way.

If you are a stomach sleeper who also has hip pain, the whole analysis shifts. Stomach sleeping with hip pain is rare, but when it occurs, the problem is usually lumbar extension (back arching) that loads the hip joint indirectly. Medium-firm is still a reasonable starting point, but the priority changes to lumbar support, not trochanter pressure.

If you change positions frequently (more than 20 position shifts per night, which is typical for most adults), the soft mattress becomes a mobility problem. It is harder to rotate your body from side to side when your hips and shoulders have sunk into a depression. Each position change requires more muscle activation, which can fragment sleep even if the mattress does not cause direct pain. The hybrid, with its coil-based responsiveness, makes position changes easier.

A Brief Note on the "You Need a Thick Mattress" Belief

Another commonly held belief among hip-pain shoppers is that thickness equals pressure relief — that a 14-inch mattress with multiple foam layers is inherently better than a thinner one. This belief also has a source with thin evidence. The original Defloor studies used mattress depths of 4 to 6 inches. There is no published research showing that a 14-inch mattress outperforms a 10-inch mattress for hip pain in healthy adults. Beyond about 3 inches of conforming top material, additional thickness adds only to the manufacturer's shipping costs and the consumer's difficulty changing sheets. Total thickness is a proxy for quality only insofar as it indicates the presence of a legitimate support layer beneath the comfort layers. A 10-inch hybrid with 2.5 inches of quality foam over 6 inches of zoned coils is almost certainly a better investment than a 14-inch all-foam mattress with 8 inches of low-density polyfoam.

The Honest Rule of Thumb

If you are a side sleeper with hip pain and you want the one purchase criterion most likely to reduce your morning pain, ignore the brand names and the online reviews and look for this: a mattress that lets your hip sink no more than the width of two stacked fingers below your spinal plane when you lie on your side. That is the gap between a surface that conforms enough and one that lets your pelvis fall into tilt. You can test it on a showroom floor or on someone else's bed in under 30 seconds. It is not a complete answer — it does not account for weight, position changes, or the specific origin of your pain — but it is a better starting point than the industry's pressure-relief marketing, and it will not cost you a night's sleep to discover.
