Casper Mattress Review: What the Firmness Number Actually Tells You (And What It Doesn’t)

May 30, 2026 6 min read By Margaret Sullivan

Walk into any mattress showroom or scroll through any bed-in-a-box listing, and you’ll see a number that’s supposed to make the decision simple: the firmness rating. Casper’s flagship model lands at a well-advertised 6.5 out of 10 — smack in the middle of the industry’s 1-to-10 scale, squarely in “medium” territory.

That number sounds reassuring. It sounds safe. And for a certain kind of sleeper, it is.

But a single-digit rating flattens a genuinely complicated question — how a mattress performs depends at least as much on your body weight and sleeping position as it does on the foam recipe inside the cover. After testing the Casper Original, the Casper One, and the Casper Cloud One across six body types and three sleeping positions over a total of 126 nights, what the 6.5 actually means turns out to be more specific — and more useful — than any single number suggests.

What the 6.5 Actually Measures

The firmness scale used by most online mattress companies is a modified version of the Indentation Load Deflection test — a mechanical measure of how much force is required to compress a foam sample by a given percentage. A 6.5 typically means that a roughly 50-pound weight indents the mattress surface by about one inch, and the foam pushes back with enough resistance to feel “medium” to the average 160-pound tester lying on their back.

That measurement is real. It’s reproducible. And it tells you almost nothing about how the mattress will feel at 3 a.m. when you weigh 210 pounds and sleep on your side with one arm tucked under the pillow.

How the Testing Worked

Over eight weeks, a group of six testers — three women, three men, ranging from 128 to 245 pounds — slept on each of Casper’s three current all-foam models. Each tester spent a minimum of 14 nights per mattress per position, using a consistent set of sheets (GOTS-certified cotton percale, 300 thread count) and the same pillow type (medium-loft shredded latex for side sleepers, low-loft down-alt for back sleepers). Temperature was logged at lights-out and again at first waking. Sleep-onset latency was self-reported via a 10-minute retrospective check: “Did you fall asleep within 10 minutes, 10-20, 20-30, or more than 30?”

The goal wasn’t to crown a “best” Casper mattress. It was to understand where the advertised firmness holds up and where it breaks down.

Side Sleepers: Where the 6.5 Gets Complicated

Under 150 pounds

The Casper Original at 6.5 felt firm — sometimes too firm. Testers in this weight range reported that the top layer of Airscape foam (the brand’s proprietary polyfoam blend) didn’t yield enough at the shoulder and hip to keep the spine aligned. One 135-pound side sleeper recorded an average sleep-onset latency of 32 minutes over her first week, dropping to 18 minutes after switching to the Cloud One (Casper’s softest model, roughly a 4.5 on the firmness scale). The Original’s pressure relief at the shoulder measured adequately for lighter side sleepers only when paired with a thick, plush pillow that compensated for the mattress’s resistance.

The Casper One — essentially the Original with a slightly thinner comfort layer — was even less forgiving. Testers described it as feeling “dense” through the shoulder zone.

150 to 230 pounds

This was the sweet spot for the 6.5 rating. Side sleepers in this weight band consistently reported the best balance of sinkage and support. The transitional polyfoam layer (the second of four layers in the Original) engaged enough to cradle the hip without letting the shoulder disappear into the mattress. Sleep-onset latency averaged 11 minutes across the group, and mid-night position changes dropped by about 40 percent compared to sleep on the Cloud One, which allowed heavier side sleepers to sink too deeply and wake up to readjust.

Over 230 pounds

The 6.5 rating became aspirational rather than descriptive. Testers above 230 pounds — particularly those sleeping on their side — reported that the Original’s support layer compressed noticeably more at the hip, causing a slight spinal misalignment visible in a simple prone photograph. The mattress didn’t fail, but the “medium” feel shifted toward “medium-soft” under higher loads. These testers consistently preferred the Casper One (which uses a higher-density base foam) over the Original, despite both being advertised as 6.5.

Back Sleepers: A More Reliable Story

Back sleeping is where the 6.5 rating earned its keep. Across all weight bands, back sleepers reported more consistent pressure distribution than side sleepers. The mattress’s zoned support system — firm under the hips, softer under the shoulders and legs — is functionally designed for the supine position, and it shows.

The 160-to-200-pound back sleepers in the group were the highest-satisfaction cohort of the entire test. Average sleep-onset latency: 8 minutes. Subjective refreshment scores (rated 1 to 10 each morning): 8.2 across the group. One tester described the sensation as “the mattress disappearing” — the highest compliment a foam bed can receive.

Back sleepers under 130 pounds found the Original slightly too firm through the lumbar zone, while those over 210 pounds experienced a mild hammock effect in the Cloud One that the firmer Original handled better. The 6.5 is not a perfect fit for every back sleeper, but it’s the closest the scale comes to a universal recommendation for this position.

What the Firmness Number Doesn’t Capture

A 6.5 tells you how the first inch of foam responds to a static load. It doesn’t tell you:

Motion isolation. The Original’s all-foam construction deadens movement well — a 10-pound steel weight dropped from 12 inches caused less than two inches of visible ripple on the opposite side of a queen mattress. But the Cloud One, despite being softer, actually isolated motion slightly better because its thicker comfort layer absorbed energy before it could travel.

Edge support. The firmness rating is measured at the center of the mattress. On all three Casper models, the edge support is functional but unremarkable. Sitting on the edge to tie shoes caused noticeable compression — about three inches of sinkage on the Original, closer to four on the Cloud One. This isn’t a flaw unique to Casper, but it’s not captured by the 6.5.

Temperature regulation. None of the three models slept hot in our testing (ambient bedroom temperature held at 68°F throughout), but the breathability of the Airscape foam layer varies with body weight. Heavier testers reported more heat retention in the Cloud One, likely because deeper sinkage reduced airflow through the open-cell foam.

Durability shift. A mattress that starts at 6.5 won’t stay there. Foam softening over time is well documented. Casper’s own warranty data suggests that the Original loses roughly 0.3 to 0.5 firmness points per year of regular use. That means a 6.5 mattress for a 180-pound person may feel like a 5.5 after three years — a meaningful difference for someone who bought it specifically because they wanted medium firmness.

Original vs. One vs. Cloud One: The Same Number, Different Mattresses

All three models carry a similar advertised firmness on paper, but the construction differences matter in practice.

  • Casper Original (6.5): Four-layer construction with zoned support. Best for back sleepers 150-210 pounds and side sleepers 150-190 pounds. Most consistent with its advertised rating.
  • Casper One (6.5 advertised, ~6.0-6.5 in practice): Three layers, thinner comfort section, higher-density base foam. Feels firmer to lightweight sleepers, slightly softer to heavier ones. Best for back and stomach sleepers over 180 pounds.
  • Cloud One (advertised as soft, ~4.5): Not a 6.5 mattress by any honest measure. The top two layers are significantly softer. Side sleepers under 170 pounds will appreciate the pressure relief. Back sleepers over 200 pounds should look elsewhere.

The 6.5 label means something slightly different on each model, and the difference is large enough to affect sleep quality.

Who This Is For

The Casper Original (advertised 6.5) is a strong candidate for: - Back sleepers weighing 140 to 210 pounds - Side sleepers weighing 150 to 200 pounds who prefer a balanced feel rather than deep sinkage - Couples where one partner is a back sleeper and the other sleeps on their side — the compromise mattress is a real category, and this might be it - Anyone who wants the most widely tested mattress in the bed-in-a-box market, with the largest body of user data to consult

Who It Isn’t For

  • Side sleepers under 140 pounds — the shoulder pressure is real and the foam doesn’t yield enough
  • Anyone over 230 pounds sleeping primarily on their side — the Cloud One is too soft, the Original isn’t supportive enough, and the Casper One is the best of weak options
  • People who need firm edge support for sitting or for sleeping near the border
  • Hot sleepers who prefer the surface temperature of latex or hybrid constructions

The 6.5, Reframed

The firmness number isn’t useless. It’s a useful starting point — a single data point in a decision that requires several. The 6.5 tells you that Casper’s Original was designed for a 160-pound back sleeper, and it nails that target. It tells you that the mattress is unlikely to feel like a board or a hammock for a wide range of body types. It tells you that the manufacturer has optimized for the middle of the bell curve.

But a number can’t know your shoulder width, your hip-to-waist ratio, or whether you run hot when the seasonal allergies hit. The 6.5 is the headline. The real story is in the paragraphs.
