CBT-I vs. Sleep Hygiene vs. Doing Nothing: What Actually Changed My Sleep

May 22, 2026 5 min read By Christopher Weston

For about two years I collected sleep hygiene tips the way other people collect kitchen gadgets. Blackout curtains. Cool room, quiet room, dark room. No coffee after 2 p.m. No phone in bed. I did all of it, sometimes for weeks at a stretch, and my sleep stayed stubbornly the same: 20 to 30 minutes to fall asleep, one or two mid-night wakings, and a 6:30 a.m. alarm that felt like a small violent event. Eventually I stopped trying, and that was worse.

I wanted to know whether the clinical treatment I kept reading about — CBT-I, or cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia — would actually deliver different results, or whether it was just the same advice wearing a more expensive coat. So I ran a three-week experiment, one condition per week, with the same bed, same room temperature (68°F), same wake time (6:30 a.m.), and a sleep log I actually stuck to.

The three conditions: - Week 1: Strict sleep hygiene — 30-minute screen cutoff, consistent wind-down ritual, no clock-watching - Week 2: No intervention — normal evening habits, no rules - Week 3: Two core CBT-I techniques — stimulus control and sleep restriction therapy — without any formal therapist or app

I tracked sleep-onset latency (minutes to fall asleep after lights out), wake-after-sleep-onset (minutes awake during the night), and subjective refreshment on a 1–10 scale upon waking.

Week 1 — Sleep Hygiene Alone

I committed to the standard list: dim lights at 9:30, screens off at 10:00, in bed by 10:30. I read a paper book, kept the thermostat at 68, and used a white-noise machine at measured 50 dB. I did not check the time.

Results after seven nights: - Average sleep-onset latency: 22 minutes (range 15–32) - Average WASO: 11 minutes (range 0–24) - Average subjective refreshment: 4.8 / 10

Nights 3 and 5 were decent — onset around 15 minutes, one brief waking. Nights 1 and 6 were indistinguishable from my baseline. The problem was not that sleep hygiene is useless. It's that sleep hygiene treats the room, not the wiring. My environment was fine. My brain's conditioned response to the bed — alertness, planning, low-grade anxiety — was untouched.

Week 2 — No Intervention

I did whatever I felt like. Phone in bed. TV until drowsy. No fixed bedtime. This was the control condition I needed to confirm I wasn't romanticizing my baseline.

Results after seven nights: - Average sleep-onset latency: 28 minutes (range 18–42) - Average WASO: 19 minutes (range 7–38) - Average subjective refreshment: 3.2 / 10

Waking up felt worse than I remembered. By night 4 I was dreading bedtime, which is the exact pattern that turns occasional poor sleep into chronic insomnia — the bed becomes a place of performance anxiety, not rest.

Week 3 — Stimulus Control and Sleep Restriction

I read through the clinical literature on CBT-I before starting this week. The two behavioral components I chose are the ones with the strongest evidence base — more trials, larger effect sizes than cognitive therapy alone.

Stimulus control is straightforward: if you're awake in bed for more than 20 minutes, get up. Go to another room. Do something boring in low light until you feel sleepy, then return to bed. Repeat as needed. The goal is to break the association between being in bed and being awake and frustrated.

Sleep restriction therapy sounds backwards, and it is, intentionally. You limit your total time in bed to your average actual sleep time — calculated from a week of logs — then gradually expand the window as sleep efficiency (time asleep ÷ time in bed) improves above 85%. For me, that meant a 5.5-hour window (1:00 a.m. to 6:30 a.m.) for the first four nights.

Results after seven nights: - Average sleep-onset latency: 9 minutes (range 4–18) - Average WASO: 3 minutes (range 0–9) - Average subjective refreshment: 7.1 / 10

The first two nights were the hardest of the entire experiment. I was genuinely tired by 11 p.m. and had to stay awake. I got out of bed twice on night one — once at 35 minutes, once at 50 minutes — and sat in the dark living room reading a boring novel until drowsiness returned.

By night four, something shifted. I was falling asleep within 8 minutes of getting into bed. I wasn't waking up until my alarm. And the subjective feeling in the morning — the absence of grogginess — was something I hadn't experienced consistently in years. Sleep restriction causes mild sleep deprivation temporarily, and that deprivation is what rebuilds the sleep drive. It's not pleasant, but it works. The Spielman et al. (1987) 3P model explains why: predisposing factors (tendency toward light sleep) + precipitating factors (stress event) + perpetuating factors (compensatory behaviors like napping, clock-watching, staying in bed when awake) form a loop. Sleep restriction and stimulus control break the perpetuating factors, which is exactly what sleep hygiene never does.

Who This Is for and Who It Isn't

This approach is for people whose primary problem is maintenance insomnia (staying asleep) or sleep-onset insomnia (falling asleep) that has persisted for more than three months, especially if you've already tried sleep hygiene without meaningful change. The cognitive component — the "C" in CBT-I — matters most for people who lie in bed with racing thoughts or catastrophic thinking about the next day's performance. If your brain is quiet but your sleep is broken, the behavioral components alone may be sufficient.

This is not for people with untreated bipolar disorder (sleep restriction can trigger manic episodes), parasomnias like sleepwalking, or very low BMI, where even modest weight loss from sleep restriction could be unsafe. If you have sleep apnea, CBT-I may still be appropriate, but only under a provider who coordinates with your sleep study and PAP therapy. The American College of Physicians recommends CBT-I as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults, but that recommendation comes with a caveat: it works best with professional guidance. DIY approaches — like mine — can teach you the principles, but a trained provider adjusts the variables based on real-time data in ways a self-experiment cannot replicate.

What I Still Do

Nine weeks after the experiment ended, I still use the stimulus control rule. I still keep my bedtime window within a range that my actual sleep time supports, which has settled at about 7 hours. I no longer think of CBT-I as a treatment I completed. I think of it as a set of principles I maintain — the same way someone who fixed their diet doesn't go back to eating whatever they want once the weight is off. The bed is where I sleep. When I'm not sleeping there, I'm somewhere else. That single rule has been worth more to me than three years of blackout curtains ever were.
