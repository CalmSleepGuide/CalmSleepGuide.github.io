CPAP Therapy and the Search for Sleep Apnea Treatment: What the Common Advice Gets Right and Wrong

May 22, 2026 9 min read By Lucy Fairbanks

The advice a newly diagnosed sleep apnea patient hears, more or less universally, goes like this: CPAP is the gold standard. Use it every night. Most people get used to it.

This is said by sleep physicians, by online forums, by the respiratory therapist who hands you the machine. It is not bad advice. But it is incomplete advice — the kind that works very well for a subset of patients and leaves the rest feeling like they are failing at something simple.

I have spent the past several weeks reading the clinical literature on obstructive sleep apnea treatment, not as a patient but as someone trying to understand what the evidence actually says about the options. What follows is a map of that territory: where the standard advice holds, where it breaks down, and what a more honest version looks like.

Where the Common Advice Is Roughly Right

The case for CPAP as a first-line treatment rests on data that is genuinely hard to argue with.

Continuous positive airway pressure — a machine that delivers air at a constant pressure through a mask, splinting the throat open during sleep — has been studied for decades across thousands of patients. The evidence shows that, when used consistently, CPAP reduces the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI, the number of breathing interruptions per hour) to near-normal levels in the majority of people with obstructive sleep apnea. It also lowers blood pressure in hypertensive patients, reduces subjective daytime sleepiness as measured by the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, and improves scores on standardized quality-of-life instruments.

A 2019 meta-analysis in The Lancet Respiratory Medicine pooled data from over 2,500 patients and found that CPAP reduced AHI by an average of 25 events per hour — a dramatic improvement that, as of now, no oral appliance and no medication has matched in head-to-head trials. For moderate to severe OSA (AHI of 15 or higher), the device is the only intervention with this depth of evidence behind it.

The advice to use CPAP is also right in a simpler sense: the machine works physiologically. Sleep apnea is, at its core, a mechanical problem — the airway collapses during sleep because the muscles that hold it open relax too much. CPAP is a mechanical solution. It applies pneumatic splinting directly to the site of collapse. It does not require the patient to learn a new skill, to wear a custom dental device, or to undergo surgery. It works the moment you turn it on.

So the standard advice is not wrong. It is, however, incomplete in ways that matter to people who cannot make CPAP work.

Where the Advice Breaks Down

The phrase "most people get used to it" hides a messier reality.

Adherence data tells a sobering story. A 2016 study in Chest followed over 1,500 newly diagnosed CPAP users for one year. By the end of that year, only 41 percent were still using the device for at least four hours per night on 70 percent of nights — the Medicare definition of "adherent." That means roughly three out of five people in that study were, by the standard clinical measure, non-adherent.

The reasons are not trivial. Mask leak, claustrophobia, aerophagia (swallowing air), skin irritation, rainout (condensation in the tubing), noise — these add up. The machine's pressure is constant and unvarying, which is the entire point of CPAP therapy, but also its primary limitation. A person who normally exhales against the low pressure of relaxed breathing now spends the night pushing air out against a positive pressure. For some patients, this is imperceptible. For others, it is exhausting enough to drive them off therapy entirely.

This is where the standard advice starts to fail. When a patient says "I tried CPAP and I can't do it," the response should not be a reflexive "try harder." It should be: here are the alternatives, and here is the evidence for each one relative to your specific case.

The table below summarizes the four main treatment categories for obstructive sleep apnea, alongside the usual supporting interventions. These are not ranked by efficacy — efficacy depends on the individual patient's anatomy, severity, and tolerance — but by the strength of the evidence base.

Oral Appliances: The Most Common Alternative

Oral appliances — specifically mandibular advancement devices that hold the lower jaw forward during sleep — are the most widely prescribed alternative to CPAP. They are less effective on average, but their adherence rates are consistently higher in the literature, and for patients with mild to moderate OSA, the trade-off can be worthwhile.

A 2021 network meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews compared oral appliances to CPAP across 34 randomized trials. CPAP reduced AHI by a mean of 26 events per hour; oral appliances reduced it by 14. That is a clinically meaningful gap. But the same analysis found that patients randomized to oral appliances used their devices more consistently — roughly 6.5 hours per night versus 5.5 for CPAP — and reported fewer side effects related to comfort and convenience.

For a patient whose baseline AHI is 20, an oral appliance that cuts it to 6 is a meaningful treatment success, even if CPAP would have cut it to 2. The question is whether the patient's physician frames it that way.

The limitation: oral appliances work best when the collapse is primarily at the level of the tongue and soft palate. They do little for collapse at the level of the epiglottis or the nasal passages. A patient needs a dental evaluation and, ideally, a drug-induced sleep endoscopy to determine whether an oral appliance is anatomically appropriate.

Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation: The Surgical Option with the Best Evidence

For patients with moderate to severe OSA who cannot tolerate CPAP and are not good candidates for an oral appliance, hypoglossal nerve stimulation — marketed under the brand name Inspire — has emerged as the most evidence-backed surgical alternative.

The system works by implanting a small pulse generator under the skin of the chest, connected to an electrode that stimulates the hypoglossal nerve, which controls tongue protrusion. When the patient breathes in, the device delivers a gentle electrical pulse that moves the tongue forward, opening the airway.

The evidence is stronger than for any other surgical approach to OSA. The STAR trial, a multicenter prospective study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2014, reported a 68 percent reduction in AHI at 12 months and significant improvements in sleepiness and quality of life. Longer-term data from the same cohort showed durability at five years, with AHI reductions maintained and no serious device-related adverse events.

But candidacy is narrow. The FDA-approved criteria require a BMI under 35, moderate to severe OSA, and a specific pattern of airway collapse on drug-induced sleep endoscopy — specifically, collapse that is anteroposterior (front-to-back) rather than concentric. A patient with a BMI over 35 or with complete concentric collapse at the palate is not a candidate.

The Medication Question

In late 2024, the FDA approved Zepbound (tirzepatide) — a GLP-1/GIP receptor agonist originally developed for type 2 diabetes and weight management — for the treatment of moderate to severe OSA in adults with obesity. The approval was based on two Phase III trials showing that tirzepatide reduced AHI by 20 to 23.8 events per hour after 52 weeks, compared with placebo reductions of roughly 5 events per hour.

These are meaningful numbers. A drug that cuts AHI by 20 events is approaching oral-appliance territory, and for a patient who qualifies — someone with OSA and a BMI of 30 or higher — the option of a weekly injection instead of a daily mask is obviously appealing.

The caveat: tirzepatide appears to work primarily through weight loss, not through any direct effect on airway mechanics. The patients in the trials lost an average of 18 to 20 percent of their body weight. That degree of weight loss independently improves OSA — the question is whether the drug adds anything beyond what weight loss alone would achieve. The prescribing information also warns of gastrointestinal side effects, including nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, which caused discontinuation in roughly 5 to 10 percent of trial participants.

For the right patient — someone with obesity-related OSA who can tolerate the medication and commit to long-term use — tirzepatide represents the first pharmacologic option with credible evidence. It is not a standalone solution. No physician should prescribe it without also recommending CPAP or an alternative mechanical therapy in the short term.

Positional Therapy and Lifestyle Changes

These interventions are not alternatives to the treatments above. They are adjuncts, and framing them as alternatives does patients a disservice.

Positional therapy — using a device or technique to prevent sleeping on the back — works when the patient's AHI is at least twice as high in the supine position as it is on the side. Roughly half of OSA patients have some positional component. For those patients, positional therapy can reduce overall AHI by several events per hour. But it rarely normalizes it, and adherence to positional devices tends to drop after the first few months.

Weight loss is the most consequential lifestyle intervention. A 10 percent reduction in body weight is associated with a 26 to 32 percent reduction in AHI, according to data from the Sleep AHEAD study. But weight loss is slow, difficult to sustain, and for many patients, OSA will not resolve completely even with significant weight loss.

Alcohol avoidance before bed, treating nasal congestion, and improving sleep hygiene all reduce AHI by small but measurable amounts. None of them alone is sufficient for clinically significant OSA.

Who Each Option Is For

CPAP is for anyone with moderate to severe OSA who can tolerate a mask and a constant pressure. It is also for patients with mild OSA who have documented daytime impairment or cardiovascular risk factors. If you can adapt to it, it remains the single most effective intervention.

Oral appliances are for patients with mild to moderate OSA who cannot tolerate CPAP, and for patients with severe OSA who cannot tolerate CPAP and are not candidates for hypoglossal nerve stimulation. They require good dentition and a healthy temporomandibular joint.

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation is for patients with moderate to severe OSA who have failed CPAP, have a BMI under 35, and have the right collapse pattern on sleep endoscopy. It is not for patients who are unwilling or unable to undergo surgery.

Tirzepatide is for patients with moderate to severe OSA and a BMI of 30 or higher who are already pursuing weight loss. It should be considered an adjunct, not a replacement for mechanical therapy.

Positional therapy is for patients whose OSA is confirmed to be supine-predominant. It should not be the sole treatment for anyone with moderate or severe disease.

Who None of These Options Are For

A patient with mild OSA who is asymptomatic, has no cardiovascular risk factors, and has a low supine AHI may not need any active treatment beyond observation and lifestyle counseling. This is a legitimate clinical decision, supported by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines, but it is rarely discussed in the CPAP-advice literature.

A patient with central sleep apnea — where the brain fails to signal the diaphragm to breathe — should not be treated with an oral appliance or hypoglossal nerve stimulation. The standard treatment is an adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) device, which is a different category of PAP therapy altogether.

A More Honest Version of the Rule

The original advice — CPAP is the gold standard; use it every night; most people get used to it — is not wrong for the patient for whom it works. It is simply incomplete for the patient for whom it does not.

A more honest version: CPAP has the strongest evidence base of any treatment for obstructive sleep apnea, and it should be the first intervention tried for anyone with moderate to severe disease. But the evidence also shows that roughly half of patients will not adhere to it long-term, and for those patients, several alternative treatments — oral appliances, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, and in specific cases, weight-loss medication — have credible data supporting their use. The decision to pursue an alternative should be based on specific anatomical and adherence data, not on discomfort alone, but neither should discomfort be dismissed.

Put another way: the original advice oversimplifies a problem that is mechanical, behavioral, and personal all at once. The more honest version acknowledges the complexity without surrendering the rigor. That is what evidence-based sleep medicine looks like when it is not in a hurry.
