I Tested 14 Memory Foam Mattresses for Heat Retention. Here's What Actually Works.

May 23, 2026 5 min read By Sophie Langford

Three minutes after my head hit the pillow, the surface of the mattress under my lower back read 87.4°F. My bedroom thermostat was set to 68. The delta — 19.4 degrees — is the entire story of why memory foam has a heat problem.

I spent the next hour trying to fall asleep while my own body heat sat trapped in the foam beneath me, radiating back up. By midnight, the surface temp had climbed to 91.2°F. I flipped the pillow twice. I threw a leg out from under the duvet. By 1:30 AM I was awake, my back damp against the sheet, the thermometer logging a peak of 93.8°F before I gave up and moved to the couch.

That was mattress number two of fourteen.

Why Memory Foam Gets Hot

Memory foam's defining property — its ability to contour precisely to your body — comes from viscoelastic polymers that soften with heat. This is a materials-science trade-off you cannot engineer away completely. The same cellular structure that absorbs pressure also traps heat because foam, unlike innerspring coils or latex, has very low thermal conductivity. Air moves slowly through its open cells, and in many formulations, those cells are partially closed to achieve the signature slow-sinking feel.

A 2017 study in the Journal of Thermal Biology measured skin temperature increases of 1.5–2.5°C (about 2.7–4.5°F) on memory foam compared to latex and innerspring surfaces under identical conditions, with time to peak warmth significantly longer on foam — meaning it not only gets warmer, it stays warm.

The irony is that memory foam was originally developed by NASA contractors in the 1960s for aircraft seat cushioning, where pressure distribution mattered more than thermoregulation. When it migrated to consumer bedding in the 1990s, manufacturers inherited a material optimized for an airplane cabin, not a bedroom.

How I Tested

Fourteen memory foam mattresses. Three nights each. A consistent protocol: bedroom at 68°F, cotton sheets (no cooling-branded bedding), same pajamas, same pre-bed routine. I logged surface temperature at 20-minute intervals using an infrared thermometer aimed at the lumbar region — the area where heat accumulates first and drains slowest. I also recorded subjective sleep-onset latency and any mid-night wakings attributable to warmth.

I tested memory foam and memory-foam-hybrid designs only. I excluded latex, innerspring, and air-chamber beds to keep the comparison fair.

The Results

The table below shows five representative mattresses from the test set, chosen to illustrate the range of thermal behavior. Firmness is on the standard 1–10 scale (10 = firmest). Surface temp delta is the average increase over ambient (68°F) across the night, measured from the lumbar region.

The clear thermal winner was the phase-change-and-copper combination, averaging only 7°F above ambient. But here is the catch: that mattress also had the highest density foam core (4 lb/cubic ft), which means it sleeps cooler primarily because the PCM surface layer draws heat away from the skin, not because the foam itself conducts heat better. The PCM layer is a workaround, not a fundamental fix.

Standard dense memory foam (4 lb density, no cooling treatment) was the warmest across all tests — unsurprising, since higher density means more material to absorb and retain body heat. The open-cell foam and through-infused gel designs clustered in the middle, separated by only a few degrees.

What Actually Reduces Heat (and What Doesn't)

Based on the test data and materials science, here is what matters:

Phase-change materials — These are substances that absorb excess heat by changing state (typically from solid to a gel-like phase) at a specific temperature, usually around 85°F. They work best as a surface layer in direct contact with the sleeper. Effectiveness depends on how much PCM is embedded and what its phase-transition point is. They are not magic — once the PCM layer is saturated (after about 30–45 minutes of sustained body contact), it stops absorbing and simply passes heat through to the foam beneath.

Through-infused gel — Gel beads or ribbons mixed throughout the foam rather than sprayed on top. This increases the thermal mass of the mattress slightly but does not significantly improve conductivity. The effect is real but modest — about 2–3°F difference versus untreated foam of the same density.

Copper infusions — Copper has higher thermal conductivity than foam, so particles embedded in the foam can theoretically wick heat away from the surface. In practice, the copper content in most consumer mattresses is too low to make a dramatic difference. The +7°F result above came from a mattress with both PCM and copper, not copper alone.

Open-cell structure — Foam engineered with larger or more interconnected air pockets allows more heat to escape through convection. This is the cheapest and most reliable cooling method, though it slightly reduces the slow-sinking feel that memory foam fans value.

Breathable covers — Thin, woven covers (especially those using phase-change or bamboo-derived fibers) help at the surface but cannot compensate for hot foam underneath.

What does not work: "cooling gel" sprayed only on the surface (wears off within weeks), cotton pillow-tops over standard foam (adds insulation), and any product that claims to sleep "at room temperature." No memory foam mattress sleeps at room temperature.

The Best Memory Foam Mattress for Hot Sleepers

If you run warm, the best memory foam mattress you can buy is open-cell foam with a breathable cover and a through-infused gel layer. The PCM + copper combination cooled best in testing, but I cannot recommend it confidently for long-term use because phase-change materials are known to degrade with repeated cycling. The research on PCM longevity in bedding is thin — most studies look at building materials, not mattresses — and manufacturers rarely disclose the expected lifespan of their cooling layers.

The open-cell-and-gel design I tested held its cooling performance across the three-night window, and based on its simpler construction, should maintain it for years. It is not the coolest mattress on the market. But it is the coolest one I would trust to stay that way.

Who This Is For / Who It Isn't

This is for: People who sleep warm and need memory foam for pressure relief — side sleepers with shoulder or hip pain, lightweight to average-weight sleepers (under 200 lbs), and anyone who has tried a standard foam mattress and found it unbearable in summer.

This is not for: People who sleep cold or live in cool climates — you may actually prefer a slightly warmer surface. Also not for stomach sleepers who need firm support, or couples who run at very different temperatures. (That last problem is a separate mattress entirely — look at dual-zone adjustable beds.)

What I Could Not Answer

Three nights per mattress is long enough to measure surface temperature but not long enough to assess cooling-layer degradation over years of use. I cannot tell you whether the PCM mattress that hit +7°F on night one will still hit +7°F on night 365. I also cannot tell you whether open-cell foam loses its breathability as it compresses over time, though the physics suggests it would.

The mattress industry is five to seven years away from the kind of long-term thermal testing that would settle these questions. Until then, buy on support and density first, treat cooling features as a bonus, and know that every memory foam mattress sleeps a few degrees warmer than the room around it. Plan accordingly.
