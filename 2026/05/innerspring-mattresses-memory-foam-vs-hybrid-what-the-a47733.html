Innerspring Mattresses: Memory Foam vs. Hybrid — What the Test Data Actually Says

May 25, 2026 6 min read By George Wentworth

The standard advice sounds clean: memory foam isolates motion, innerspring keeps you cool and supported. It gets repeated on every mattress site, every buying guide, every thread. It is roughly correct for the two extremes. But the extreme products — a dense, slow-recovery all-foam bed on one side, a traditional open-coil innerspring on the other — are becoming hard to find in retail. What most people buy today is a hybrid. And hybrid construction changes the trade-offs enough that the old rule needs updating.

The verdict, tested over 60 logged nights across two mattress types in the same room, same pillow, same cover: for the average adult (150–200 lbs), a hybrid with a firm polyfoam comfort layer and pocketed coils outperforms both a traditional innerspring and a dense memory foam bed on the combined metrics of sleep-onset speed, temperature stability, and motion isolation. But the margin is narrow and position-dependent. The real decision isn't foam or coils. It's coil gauge, foam density, and layer thickness — specs most product pages bury in marketing copy.

How the Categories Actually Break Down

Traditional innerspring. A single layer of interconnected steel coils (Bonnel or offset), topped with a thin foam or fiber pad. The coils move as a unit. Bounce is high. Edge support is weak. Motion transfer is poor. These beds breathe extremely well because there is almost nothing between you and the air gap inside the coil cavity. They are increasingly rare online, though still common in budget hotels and older guest rooms.

Modern hybrid. Pocketed coils (each spring wrapped in fabric, moving independently), topped with one or more layers of foam — typically polyfoam, sometimes memory foam or latex. The coil gauge, coil count, and foam density vary wildly. A hybrid with thin, low-density foam can feel nearly identical to a traditional innerspring. A hybrid with thick, high-density memory foam on top of firm coils can feel nearly identical to an all-foam bed. The label tells you almost nothing.

All-foam / memory foam. No coils. Support comes from high-density polyfoam base layers; comfort comes from a top layer of viscoelastic (memory) foam or latex. Motion isolation is excellent. Temperature regulation is poor unless the foam is specially formulated (gel-infused, open-cell, copper, etc.) or the design includes air channels. Edge support is weak.

My Test Setup

Two mattress constructions, tested sequentially in the same bedroom. Room temperature held between 19°C and 21°C (66–70°F). Bedtime logged: target 23:00, actual recorded. Wake time: recorded at first morning bathroom trip, then at final alarm. Sleep-onset latency: estimated from the 10-minute check-in method (check time, close eyes, if not asleep in ~10 min, check again). Mid-night wakings: logged via bathroom trips and subjective recall. Subjective refreshment: 1–10 scale logged within 30 minutes of waking.

Mattress A — Traditional innerspring (bought used from a guest room; coil count unknown, Bonnel-style, ~3 cm fiber pad on top, no foam). Tested 30 nights.

Mattress B — Hybrid (pocketed coils, 13.5 gauge, 15 cm; 5 cm medium-firm polyfoam comfort layer; no memory foam). Tested 30 nights. I also include notes from a 12-month stretch on a dense all-foam bed (three-layer memory foam, ~12 cm total, medium firmness) tested two years prior.

The Numbers

Perceived temperature (1 = cold, 10 = sweat) 2 4 7
Edge support (sit test, 1 = collapse, 10 = solid) 4 8 3

These are single-subject data. Interpret accordingly. But they track closely with published findings on the biomechanics of mattress construction: coil-based support reduces spinal load in supine sleepers compared to foam-only designs (Low et al., 2017, Applied Ergonomics); and polyfoam comfort layers provide a useful middle ground between the pressure relief of memory foam and the responsiveness of latex or fiber padding (Jacobson et al., 2010, Journal of Chiropractic Medicine).

Where the Common Advice Works

Motion isolation. The foam beds (hybrid and all-foam) killed partner disturbance. The innerspring transmitted every shift. If you sleep with someone who moves, you do not want a traditional innerspring. The hybrid with pocketed coils was good enough that my partner's nighttime bathroom trips (2–3 per night, logged) woke me on only 3 of 30 nights. The all-foam woke me on 0 of 30 during its testing period.

Edge support. The hybrid was the clear winner. The pocketed coils are reinforced at the perimeter in most modern designs, and I could sit on the edge to tie shoes without sliding off. The innerspring sagged immediately. The all-foam compressed to about 60% of its center height. If you sit on the edge of your bed daily, a hybrid is the practical choice.

Where It Breaks Down

Cooling. The advice says innerspring runs cool. True, but the hybrid was not far behind. The temperature difference between the innerspring and hybrid nights was perceptible — the innerspring felt genuinely chilly on 19°C nights — but it never caused sweating on either mattress. The all-foam was a different story: I woke with damp sheets roughly twice a week during summer months, even with percale cotton sheets. The cooling gap is real between foam and coil-based beds. Between innerspring and hybrid, it's marginal for most sleepers in a temperate room.

Weight tiers. The old advice treats all sleepers as the same. They are not. At 175 lbs, I found the innerspring supportive enough for back sleeping but inadequate for side sleeping — my hip sank past the fiber pad and contacted the coils directly, causing pressure soreness by morning. The same side-sleeping position on the hybrid was comfortable because the polyfoam layer (5 cm) provided enough contour without bottoming out. A sleeper under 130 lbs would likely find the hybrid too firm and prefer a thicker comfort layer. A sleeper over 220 lbs would likely need a higher coil gauge (lower number = thicker wire) than my hybrid's 13.5 to avoid sagging. The category name tells you nothing about these thresholds. The spec sheet does.

Sleep position. Back sleepers get the most benefit from coil support because the spine is in neutral alignment with minimal contouring needed. Stomach sleepers need enough firmness to keep the hips from sinking into hyperextension — the hybrid's polyfoam plus coils did this well; the innerspring did it fine; the all-foam was slightly too soft. Side sleepers need pressure relief at the shoulder and hip, which requires a comfort layer thick enough to contour but not so thick that the hips sink out of alignment. That Goldilocks zone is not predicted by "foam vs. coils." It is predicted by the interaction of body weight, comfort layer density, and coil response.

Who This Is For / Who It Isn't

This matters for you if: You are between 140 and 210 lbs, sleep on your back or side, and share a bed. A hybrid with pocketed coils and a medium-firm polyfoam (not memory foam) comfort layer is likely the best single compromise across all metrics. You give up almost nothing on cooling, gain significant motion isolation over a traditional innerspring, and avoid the heat retention and edge sag of all-foam.

It matters less if: You sleep alone, sleep hot, and sleep on your back. A traditional innerspring or a firm hybrid will work about equally well; you are optimizing for temperature and support, not motion isolation, and both deliver.

It does not apply if: You are under 130 lbs or over 230 lbs. The comfort layer thickness and coil gauge that worked for my weight class will not work for yours. Lightweight side sleepers need a softer comfort layer (or memory foam) to achieve contouring. Heavy sleepers need a higher coil gauge and/or a firmer foam base to prevent bottoming out. The category is irrelevant; the specs are everything.

A More Honest Rule

Replace memory foam for motion isolation, innerspring for support with this: Match your support layer (coils or high-density foam) to your weight. Match your comfort layer (memory foam, polyfoam, or latex) to your sleep position and temperature sensitivity. If you side-sleep and share a bed, pocketed coils with a polyfoam or latex top layer will likely outperform both all-foam and traditional innerspring on the mixed criteria that actually determine sleep quality. If you back-sleep alone, the category barely matters — get the firmness right and the rest follows.

The hybrid is not a compromise. It's a separate category with its own performance profile. The old advice assumed a binary that no longer describes the market.

What I Sleep On

After these 60 nights, I kept the hybrid. Not because it scored highest on every axis — the all-foam was better for motion isolation, the innerspring was cooler — but because it was the only mattress that never made me think about the mattress. I fell asleep faster, woke less, and stopped logging complaints. That is the best evidence I have that the logic works: I stopped needing the data because the bed stopped being a variable.
