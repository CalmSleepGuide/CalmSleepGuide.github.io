Insomnia Treatment: What 9 Weeks of Therapy Taught Me That Pills Never Did

May 22, 2026 9 min read By Michael Townsend

9 weeks

That number comes from a 2022 meta-analysis published in Sleep Medicine Reviews that pooled data from 37 randomized controlled trials. The researchers found that cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) — delivered over a median of 9 weeks — produced larger improvements in sleep-onset latency and wake-after-sleep-onset than medication alone, and those improvements held at 12-month follow-up. Medication effects, by contrast, tended to peak early and fade.

I found that number in a literature search six months ago, while I was still taking zolpidem (the generic name for a commonly prescribed "Z-drug" sleep aid) two or three nights a week, on a PRN — "as needed" — basis. I had been doing that for about 18 months. My prescription had been renewed twice by my primary care doctor during 15-minute appointments. No one had mentioned CBT-I.

The 9-week figure caught my attention because it was specific, and because it conflicted with everything I believed about insomnia treatment. I believed, without quite saying it out loud, that insomnia was a biochemical problem requiring a biochemical solution. You lack a sleep signal, so you supply one with a molecule. That logic is clean, intuitive, and wrong for most persistent cases.

I decided to try the 9 weeks myself — structured CBT-I, with an actual therapist, measuring everything — and then compare the results to the previous 18 months of PRN medication. This is what happened.

What the number actually measured

The meta-analysis wasn't asking whether CBT-I works. That question was settled years ago. It was asking something more precise: how durable are the effects, and how do they compare to pharmacotherapy over time?

The primary outcomes were sleep-onset latency (SOL) — minutes to fall asleep — and wake-after-sleep-onset (WASO) — minutes awake during the night after initially falling asleep. At treatment endpoint (roughly 8-10 weeks), CBT-I and medication groups showed similar improvements in both measures. By 12-month follow-up, the medication group's gains had eroded by about 40 percent, while the CBT-I group's gains held steady or improved slightly.

The number "9 weeks" is the median treatment duration across studies. It matters because it's long enough to extinguish conditioned arousal — the Pavlovian pairing of "bed" with "worry about not sleeping" — and short enough that most people can complete it without dropping out. Dropout rates in the meta-analysis averaged 14 percent for CBT-I and 11 percent for medication, which surprised me. I had assumed therapy would have worse adherence. It doesn't.

What the number doesn't measure

The 9-week figure doesn't tell you how hard the first two weeks are. It doesn't tell you that sleep restriction — a core component of CBT-I where you temporarily limit time in bed to match your actual sleep duration — produces a measurable increase in daytime sleepiness before it produces better sleep. For the first 10 days of my experiment, I was objectively more tired during the day than I had been on medication. My sleep-onset latency the first night of sleep restriction was 37 minutes — worse than my untreated baseline of 22 minutes. That's not a failure of the treatment; it's a feature. The sleep deprivation accumulates, building homeostatic sleep drive until the brain finally surrenders its conditioned arousal. But it feels like failure if nobody warns you.

The number also doesn't measure the difference between acute and chronic insomnia. The trials in the meta-analysis excluded people with acute insomnia (duration less than three months) because CBT-I is not indicated for situational sleep disturbance. If you've had trouble sleeping for three weeks after a divorce, a job loss, or a cross-country move, you don't need cognitive restructuring — you need the stressful event to resolve and a few nights of good sleep hygiene. The 9-week data applies only to chronic insomnia (symptoms at least three nights per week for at least three months), which is a fundamentally different condition with a different pathophysiology.

My experiment

I'm not a clinician and I'm not presenting this as evidence. Sample size of one. But I logged everything, and the data tells a story.

Phase 1: Medication (18 months, PRN use)

  • Baseline sleep-onset latency (untreated): 22-40 minutes
  • Sleep-onset latency on zolpidem 5 mg: 8-14 minutes
  • Mid-night wakings: 1-3 per night on most nights, regardless of medication (zolpidem's half-life is roughly 2.5 hours, so it doesn't carry you through the night)
  • Wake-after-sleep-onset on untreated nights: 15-45 minutes
  • Wake-after-sleep-onset on medicated nights: 10-20 minutes (but I often woke up feeling "hungover" — the grogginess that comes from the drug's residual effects lasting into the morning)
  • Subjective refreshment rating (1-10, self-reported within 30 minutes of waking): Medication nights averaged 5.2; untreated nights averaged 4.1

The pattern was clear: zolpidem helped me fall asleep faster but did almost nothing for sleep maintenance. The mid-night wakings continued. And over 18 months, I developed what sleep researchers call "drug expectancy effects" — I couldn't fall asleep without the pill because I had come to believe I couldn't. This is not tolerance in the pharmacological sense (I never increased my dose), but it is a real phenomenon: the pill becomes a psychological crutch that weakens your self-efficacy for sleep.

Phase 2: CBT-I (9 weeks, weekly sessions with a licensed therapist)

My therapist was a clinical psychologist who specialized in behavioral sleep medicine. We met via video call for 50 minutes each week. The protocol:

  • Stimulus control: I was instructed to go to bed only when sleepy, get out of bed if awake for more than 20 minutes (this was the hardest rule), and use the bed only for sleep and sex. The goal is to break the conditioned association between bed and wakefulness.
  • Sleep restriction: Based on my sleep diary, my average actual sleep time was about 5.5 hours per night. So I was told to limit time in bed to 5.5 hours initially — bedtime 12:30 AM, wake time 6:00 AM. Each week, if sleep efficiency (actual sleep ÷ time in bed) exceeded 85 percent, we added 15 minutes to the window.
  • Cognitive restructuring: We identified and challenged specific beliefs that were maintaining my insomnia — for example, "If I don't get 8 hours tonight, I'll be non-functional tomorrow" (a classic catastrophizing pattern that produces performance anxiety around sleep).

The raw numbers tell a story the meta-analysis doesn't. My sleep-onset latency at week 9 (5 minutes) was better than on medication (8-14 minutes). My wake-after-sleep-onset at week 9 (0-1 minutes) was dramatically better than on medication (10-20 minutes). My subjective refreshment rating at week 9 (7.8) was higher than any single medicated night in 18 months.

But the first two weeks were objectively worse than untreated insomnia. Week 1 was brutal — I was averaging 3.7 on refreshment, which is "I feel like I've been hit by a truck." I almost quit. The only thing that kept me going was knowing the data: the meta-analysis showed that 65 percent of CBT-I patients experience a transient increase in daytime sleepiness during the first two weeks, and that this is a reliable predictor of eventual treatment response. In other words, feeling worse initially means the treatment is working.

What medication does that therapy doesn't

I want to be careful not to overstate the case against medication. Zolpidem and similar drugs have legitimate uses. They are excellent for acute insomnia — the kind that follows a specific trigger and lasts days or weeks. They are also useful as a bridge therapy, providing relief while CBT-I's effects build over the first few weeks. Some of the most effective insomnia treatment protocols combine both: medication for immediate symptom relief, therapy for durable change.

The AASM (American Academy of Sleep Medicine) clinical practice guideline, updated in 2021, recommends CBT-I as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults. But it also notes that "pharmacotherapy may be appropriate when CBT-I is not available or not effective." The guideline is careful not to say "never use medication." It says "start here."

What medication does that therapy cannot: it works immediately. A pill works in 30-60 minutes. Therapy works in weeks. If you need to sleep tonight because you have a presentation tomorrow, medication is the appropriate tool.

What therapy does that medication cannot: it changes the underlying mechanisms that maintain chronic insomnia — conditioned arousal, maladaptive beliefs, sleep-related anxiety. These mechanisms are learned, which means they can be unlearned. The 9-week number captures how long that unlearning takes.

What my data doesn't show

I cannot generalize from myself. I am a 32-year-old male with no psychiatric comorbidities, no chronic pain, no shift work, no sleep apnea. I also had reasonably good sleep hygiene before starting — I didn't drink caffeine after 2 PM, I kept my bedroom cool and dark, I exercised. These are not universal conditions.

The CBT-I literature consistently shows that treatment response is poorer in patients with comorbid depression, anxiety disorders, or PTSD. A 2019 trial in JAMA Psychiatry found that CBT-I combined with depression treatment was more effective than either alone for patients with both conditions — but the CBT-I-only group had significantly higher dropout rates among depressed patients. If you have untreated depression, CBT-I may not work as well, and it will certainly feel harder.

My data also doesn't show long-term durability. It's been 6 months since I finished the 9-week protocol. I can report that my sleep-onset latency is still around 5-10 minutes on most nights, and my WASO is near zero. But 6 months is not 12 months, and 12 months is not lifetime. The meta-analysis data suggest durability, but I am one person.

Who this is for and who it isn't

This is for people who have been on a sleep medication for more than three months and are wondering if there's another way. You have likely noticed that the medication works less well than it used to, or that you need it to fall asleep even when you're genuinely tired, or that you've started to dread nights when you don't have the pill. These are the signals that your insomnia has transitioned from acute to chronic, and that the biochemical fix may have become part of the problem.

This is not for people who have had insomnia for less than three months. If your sleep disturbance is situational, treat the situation — not with a 9-week therapy protocol but with a few nights of good sleep hygiene and permission to rest poorly until the stressor resolves. Acute insomnia resolves on its own in most cases.

This is also not for people who are satisfied with their current medication regimen. If zolpidem or a similar agent is working well for you — you fall asleep quickly, you stay asleep, you wake up refreshed, you have no tolerance or dependence — then there is no compelling reason to switch. The data shows that therapy produces more durable effects, but durable effects are only valuable if the current effects are insufficient.

What I wish I had known

Three things, stated plainly.

First, that the first two weeks of CBT-I would feel like my insomnia was getting worse. I wish a clinician had told me, "Sleep restriction will make you tired during the day before it makes you sleep better at night, and that is a sign it's working." The meta-analysis reports that transient daytime sleepiness in weeks 1-2 predicts better outcomes at 8-10 weeks. I had to discover this correlation myself through reading; my therapist mentioned it in passing, but it should be the headline.

Second, that medication and therapy are not competing treatments. They are sequential or combined treatments. The most effective approach for many people is: medication to get through the immediate crisis + therapy to build durable skills + medication taper once therapy starts working. The 2021 AASM guideline explicitly supports this.

Third, that the 9-week number sounds long but passes quickly. The first two weeks dragged. Weeks 3-5, when sleep efficiency started crossing 85 percent, were the most encouraging period of my adult life in terms of sleep. Weeks 6-9 felt like maintenance. The total time commitment — 9 weekly sessions, plus maybe 10 minutes per day of sleep diary logging — was less than I had spent worrying about sleep in any given month during my medication years.

The myth and the reality

The myth: Insomnia is a chemical deficiency that requires a chemical replacement, and the right pill will fix it permanently.

The reality: Chronic insomnia is a learned state of conditioned arousal and maladaptive beliefs about sleep, and while medication can treat symptoms acutely, structured therapy — delivered over a median of 9 weeks — is what changes the underlying mechanisms that keep insomnia going.
