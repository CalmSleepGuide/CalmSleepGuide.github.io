Mattress Comparison: Two Beds, One Lab, 30 Nights

May 26, 2026 4 min read By Robert Halstead

A mattress comparison between two all-foam beds from major direct-to-consumer brands should be straightforward: same construction approach, similar price point, both marketed as "medium firm." After 30 nights on each, the real difference isn't in the specs — it's in how they sleep, and that difference is large enough to send you toward the wrong bed if you shop by description alone.

The short verdict: The bed that feels better in the first five minutes is not the bed that sleeps better on night 30. Pick the one that sleeps better, not the one that sinks softer.

How We Got Here

The bed-in-a-box category convinced us, over the past decade, that foam mattresses are essentially interchangeable once you match the firmness number. A 6 out of 10 on the firmness scale from Brand A should feel about the same as a 6 from Brand B. That belief has a source: the mattress industry's adoption of a 1-10 firmness scale, which was never a standardized measuring tool. It was a marketing shorthand that retailers and manufacturers used independently, each calibrating to their own reference point. That means a 6 from one company may be a 4 from another. The number creates the illusion of comparability where none exists.

This comparison tests that assumption directly — two beds, both claiming medium firm, same room, same sleeper, same pillow and sheets, 30 nights each.

The Beds

Mattress A uses a 2-inch gel memory foam comfort layer over a 6-inch polyfoam support core. The cover is polyester-rayon blend with a quilted surface. Total height: 10 inches.

Mattress B uses a 1.5-inch proprietary polyfoam comfort layer (no memory foam), a 1.5-inch transition polyfoam layer, and a 6-inch polyfoam support core. The cover is a polyester-spandex knit, not quilted. Total height: 10 inches.

On paper, the difference is subtle — half an inch of foam type. In practice, it's everything.

## The Test Protocol

Nights 1–30: Mattress A, followed immediately by 30 nights on Mattress B. Same room temperature target (68°F), same bedtime window (10:30–11:00 PM), same wake time (6:30 AM). No alcohol on test nights. Sleep tracked via a readiness wearable and a handwritten log of subjective quality (1–10).

Measured Results

Metric Mattress A Mattress B
Avg sleep-onset latency (nights 1–5) 18 min 24 min
Avg sleep-onset latency (nights 20–30) 11 min 8 min
Mid-night wakings (avg per night) 1.2 0.7
Subjective refreshment (1–10 avg) 6.8 8.2
Night sweats reported 7 of 30 2 of 30

Mattress A felt better immediately. The gel memory foam gives a slow, contouring sink on first contact — the "ahh" effect that showroom floors are designed to produce. But that initial comfort came with a thermal cost. By night seven, I was waking once or twice most nights, often slightly damp. The quilted cover and dense memory foam layer trap heat more than the specs suggest. Sleep-onset latency improved over time as I adjusted, but the mid-night disruption never resolved.

Mattress B felt worse for the first week. The polyfoam comfort layer is firmer on contact — less hug, more resistance. Nights three through five were the worst: I logged a 6 on refreshment and clocked 26 minutes to fall asleep. Around night nine, something shifted. The transition layer (the middle 1.5-inch slab that Mattress A lacks) started doing its job — providing pressure relief without the sinking sensation. By week three, sleep-onset dropped to 8 minutes and stayed there. The knit cover breathes noticeably better.

This is the part that matters: the bed that felt worse on day one slept better on day 30.

Who Each Bed Is For

Mattress A works for side sleepers under 180 pounds who run cool at night and prioritize that initial pressure-relief feeling. The memory foam cradle is real and effective for shoulder and hip comfort — if you don't overheat easily. It is not a good fit for stomach sleepers, whose hips tended to sink past the 2-inch comfort layer into the firmer support core, creating a mild hammock effect.

Mattress B works better for back sleepers, combination sleepers, and anyone who sleeps warm. The faster-response foam makes changing positions easier — there's no "stuck" feeling when you roll from back to side. The edge support is also noticeably firmer, which matters if you share the bed or sit on the edge while getting dressed. Stomach sleepers at moderate weight (under 200) stayed in better alignment on this bed.

Who neither works for: Anyone over 220 pounds. Both beds are 10 inches of all-foam construction, which is serviceable but not supportive enough to prevent midsection sinking at higher weights. A hybrid (coil-on-foam) construction would serve better.

The Number We Opened With

The industry told us two medium-firm beds are comparable. They are not. The firmness scale is a reference point calibrated independently by each manufacturer — it tells you where the bed sits within that brand's own lineup, not where it sits in the market. A 6 from Mattress A and a 6 from Mattress B are two different experiences separated by 30 nights of data.

What the scale can't capture is the thing that matters most: whether the bed sleeps cool, whether the foam keeps you on top of it or lets you sink in, and whether the comfort layer holds up across a full night or only the first four hours. Those variables are invisible on a showroom floor and absent from a spec sheet. The only way to find them is to sleep on the bed long enough for the initial impression to fade — which is why the sleep trial exists, and why using it is the most important part of any mattress comparison.
