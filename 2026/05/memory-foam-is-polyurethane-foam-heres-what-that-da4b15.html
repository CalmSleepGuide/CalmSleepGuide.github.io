Memory Foam Is Polyurethane Foam. Here's What That Actually Means.

May 26, 2026 4 min read By Katherine Henley

Walk into any mattress showroom and you'll hear the same claim: this bed has memory foam. It's said like it means something — a material category as distinct as wood or steel. Here's what it actually means: memory foam is polyurethane foam. Between 93 and 97 percent of it, by weight, is plain polyurethane. The remaining 3 to 7 percent is the chemical additives that give it the properties mattress shoppers are actually paying for.

That number — 93 to 97 percent — comes from a 2014 review in the Journal of Cellular Plastics that examined how viscoelastic polyurethane foam is formulated. It matters because it resets the conversation. You aren't choosing between memory foam and polyurethane foam. You're choosing between polyurethane foams with different additive packages. The question isn't what it's made of. It's what those additives do, and whether the result helps you sleep.

What the NASA Story Actually Tells You

Every memory foam explainer mentions NASA, and for good reason. Charles Yost and his team at Stencel Aero Engineering developed a "temper foam" in 1966 for aircraft crash protection. It was a polyurethane foam engineered to deform under pressure and return to shape slowly — useful for absorbing the force of impact without bouncing the pilot's head back off the seat.

That slow recovery is the key. Standard polyurethane foam — the kind in couch cushions and mattress base layers — bounces back fast. Push your hand into it, and it springs back when you lift. Memory foam doesn't. It resists immediate recovery, which is a property called viscoelasticity. The foam is both viscous (it resists flow) and elastic (it can return to its original shape). The ratio of those two qualities is what determines how a given foam feels.

Temperature affects that ratio directly. Warm foam becomes more elastic and returns to shape faster. Cool foam stiffens and recovers more slowly. This is why some people find memory foam comfortable in summer and stifling in winter — the same material is physically different at different room temperatures. NASA didn't design for bedroom comfort. That 3 to 7 percent additive package was retrofitted for sleep by mattress manufacturers in the 1990s, and the compromises are still baked in.

The One Word That Explains Everything

There's a technical term that explains almost everything you need to know about memory foam, and almost no mattress advertisement uses it: hysteresis.

Hysteresis describes the lag between applying force to a material and the material returning to its original shape. In foam terms, it's the energy lost as heat during compression. Memory foam has high hysteresis. Standard polyurethane foam has low hysteresis. When you lie on a high-hysteresis foam, the foam absorbs kinetic energy and dissipates it as heat rather than pushing back against you.

This is what people mean when they say memory foam "cradles" or "hugs." It's not a vague marketing sentiment. It's a measurable physical property — the foam's inability to return energy to your body. That's why pressure points (shoulders, hips, heels) sink in and feel supported. The foam isn't pushing back.

It's also why memory foam sleeps hot. The dissipated energy has to go somewhere, and it goes into the foam as heat. Standard polyurethane foam stores less energy because it releases it mechanically — it bounces back. Memory foam traps that same energy as warmth.

What Mattress Labels Don't Say About Density

When a mattress tag lists "3-pound memory foam," it's referring to density — pounds of foam per cubic foot. Density is one of the few quantifiable specs you can use to compare foams across brands, and it directly predicts durability. A 2016 study in Polymer Testing found that higher-density viscoelastic foams retained more of their original support after repeated compression cycles. Higher density means longer lifespan.

But density doesn't tell you how the foam feels. Two foams at the same density can behave completely differently because density measures mass, not firmness. Firmness is measured by IFD (indentation force deflection) — the pounds of force required to compress the foam by a certain percentage. A high-density foam can be soft. A low-density foam can be firm. The two numbers describe different properties.

What you actually want, if you're shopping: a density of at least 3 to 4 pounds for the memory foam layers closest to your body, and an IFD number you'd need to ask the manufacturer for because most mattress tags omit it. If a company can't or won't tell you the IFD of its foam, you're buying blind.

The Trade-Off No Tag Will Print

The open-cell vs. closed-cell distinction appears in foam marketing as a solution to the heat problem. Open-cell foams have interconnected air pockets that allow more airflow. Closed-cell foams have sealed bubbles that trap heat. Most mattress memory foams today are open-cell, and many are infused with gel, copper, or phase-change materials to pull heat away from the body.

These work. They also change the feel. Gel infusions, particularly, stiffen the foam and reduce hysteresis. You lose some of that slow-cradling sensation in exchange for cooler sleep. There is no perfect foam that is simultaneously highly viscoelastic, highly breathable, and highly durable. Every formulation sacrifices one quality to improve another.

This is the central tension. Memory foam that hugs you deeply will sleep warmer. Memory foam that sleeps cool will have less of that distinctive slow-sink feel. Memory foam that lasts fifteen years will be dense and heavy. Memory foam that is lightweight and easy to move will break down faster. You don't get to choose which trade-offs exist. You only get to choose which ones you're willing to live with.

Who It Helps, Who It Doesn't

Memory foam works best for people who sleep on their side or have bony pressure points (shoulders, hips) that need cushioning. It works poorly for people who sleep hot, people who change positions frequently throughout the night (the slow recovery makes turning feel like moving through mud), and people who prefer a firm, responsive surface that doesn't sink.

It is not a universal upgrade over innerspring or latex. It is a specific tool for a specific problem — pressure relief — with measurable costs: heat retention, delayed response, and weight.

A Rule of Thumb

Before you buy a memory foam mattress, lie on it for at least ten minutes in your usual sleep position, then ask yourself whether you want to feel held or to feel like you're floating — because memory foam can only deliver one of those honestly, and it can't fake the other.
