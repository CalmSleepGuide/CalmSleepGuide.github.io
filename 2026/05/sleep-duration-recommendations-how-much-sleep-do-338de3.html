Sleep Duration Recommendations: How Much Sleep Do Different Age Groups Actually Need?

May 26, 2026 6 min read By Grace Ellerton

You've probably asked yourself this at some point in the last month: Am I getting enough sleep?

Maybe you're the parent of a toddler who wakes at 5:30 a.m. no matter what. Maybe you're an adult in your forties who gets seven hours but spends the afternoon fighting drowsiness. Or maybe you're over sixty-five, sleeping six and a half hours, waking once or twice, and wondering whether that's normal or a problem in progress.

The confusion is understandable. Sleep duration recommendations appear everywhere — eight hours is the magic number, except when it's seven to nine, except when you're older and the guidelines shift again. The internet offers definitive-sounding charts with exact hour counts for every age bracket, but those charts inevitably leave you with the same question: Which number is my number?

Let's start with what the consensus actually says, then walk through what it means for a real person.

The Standard Recommendations — and Why They're Only Half the Story

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, and the Sleep Research Society all publish age-stratified sleep duration guidelines. The ranges look roughly like this:

Age group Recommended daily sleep What's common but not ideal
Newborns (0–3 months) 14–17 hours 11–13 or 18–19 hours
Infants (4–11 months) 12–15 hours 10–11 or 16–18 hours
Toddlers (1–2 years) 11–14 hours 9–10 or 15–16 hours
Preschoolers (3–5) 10–13 hours 8–9 or 14 hours
School-age (6–13) 9–11 hours 7–8 or 12 hours
Teenagers (14–17) 8–10 hours 7 or 11 hours
Adults (18–64) 7–9 hours 6 or 10 hours
Older adults (65+) 7–8 hours 5–6 or 9 hours

These are useful as reference points. They are not prescriptions.

A 2017 study in Sleep Health that helped shape these guidelines noted something important: the ranges represent the sleep duration associated with optimal health outcomes at a population level, not a guarantee that any individual will feel restored at the low end or the high end of the range. The guidelines are a starting gate, not a finish line.

The Self-Assessment That Matters More Than the Clock

Here's where the age-based chart falls short. Two adults of the same age, same sex, same general fitness level can have vastly different sleep needs. One feels sharp after seven hours. The other feels hungover after eight and a half. Why?

The research points to a mix of genetics (a small percentage of the population are natural short sleepers carrying a mutation in the DEC2 gene), sleep architecture differences, and — most commonly — sleep continuity.

Before you fixate on whether you're hitting the exact hour count for your age group, ask yourself these four questions:

  1. Do you need an alarm clock to wake up? Not a backup alarm. Your first alarm. If you consistently cannot wake naturally at your intended time, you may be sleeping less than your body requires.
  2. Do you rely on caffeine to function before noon? Occasional use is normal. Daily dependence — where you can't think clearly without it — is a sign that your sleep is not meeting your needs.
  3. Do you sleep two or more hours longer on weekends or days off? This is sometimes called "sleep debt repayment." If you consistently need catch-up sleep, your weekday duration is likely insufficient.
  4. Do you feel drowsy during sedentary activities? Falling asleep reading, watching a movie, or sitting in a meeting is a stronger signal of insufficient sleep than most people realize.

If you answered yes to two or more of these, the exact hour recommendation for your age group matters less than the fact that your current sleep pattern is not recovering you adequately.

The Mechanism Gap: Why Duration Alone Is Misleading

When experts talk about sleep duration recommendations, they're really talking about an opportunity window. Seven hours in bed does not equal seven hours of sleep. And seven hours of fragmented sleep is not the same as seven hours of continuous sleep.

Sleep architecture changes across the lifespan, and this is where age-group guidelines start to show their limits.

Deep sleep (N3 slow-wave sleep) declines steadily from young adulthood onward. A twenty-five-year-old might spend 20 percent of the night in deep sleep. A sixty-five-year-old might spend less than 5 percent. This is normal, but it means an older adult's seven hours contains significantly less of the most physically restorative sleep stage than a younger adult's seven hours.

REM sleep, which is critical for memory consolidation and emotional regulation, also shifts with age — but more gradually. The real change is fragmentation. As people age, they experience more spontaneous awakenings, lighter sleep, and more time spent in stage N1 (the drowsy borderline between wake and sleep). Someone who logs eight hours in bed but has four or five awakenings longer than five minutes may get less restorative sleep than someone who logs six and a half hours of solid, uninterrupted sleep.

This is why the sleep duration recommendations for older adults (7–8 hours) are slightly lower than for younger adults (7–9 hours). The recommendation acknowledges that sleep efficiency — the percentage of time in bed actually spent asleep — naturally declines with age. The number is lower because the quality per hour is, on average, lower.

What Actually Happens When You Don't Get Enough

The consequences of chronically insufficient sleep are well-documented and worth understanding in plain terms.

Cognitive performance declines measurably after even one night of restricted sleep. A landmark 2003 study by Van Dongen and colleagues found that people sleeping six hours per night for fourteen consecutive days showed cognitive deficits equivalent to two nights of total sleep deprivation. Importantly, the participants did not feel proportionally impaired — their subjective sleepiness ratings plateaued while their objective performance continued to deteriorate. You can adapt to feeling tired without adapting to being cognitively compromised.

Beyond cognition, chronic short sleep is associated with increased allostatic load — the physiological wear and tear that accumulates when the body's stress systems are repeatedly activated. This shows up as elevated evening cortisol, impaired glucose tolerance, increased sympathetic nervous system activity, and higher baseline inflammation markers like C-reactive protein. These changes don't announce themselves as "I'm not sleeping enough." They announce themselves as brain fog, slow recovery from illness, weight that won't shift, and a vague sense of not bouncing back the way you used to.

The Tricky Case of Older Adults

If you're over sixty-five and sleeping six and a half hours without daytime symptoms, the evidence does not support forcing yourself to sleep longer. The National Sleep Foundation's 2015 consensus report explicitly notes that while the recommended range for older adults is 7–8 hours, some healthy older adults function well on 5–7 hours, and the key indicator is not the number but the absence of daytime impairment.

What is worth addressing in this age group is not the duration but the fragmentation. Frequent nocturnal awakenings, early morning awakening without being able to return to sleep, and increased time spent awake during the night are not inevitable — they are often treatable. Addressing obstructive sleep apnea, adjusting medication timing, and managing nocturia can substantially improve sleep continuity without changing the total time in bed.

What This Means for Parents

If you're a parent reading this, the sleep duration recommendations for your child are a useful benchmark, but they need to be held loosely. A toddler who sleeps ten hours and wakes happy is fine, even if the chart says eleven to fourteen. A teenager who sleeps nine hours but fights wake-up every morning and crashes on weekends is likely underslept, even if nine hours falls within the recommended range.

Watch the behavior, not the clock. Mood, attention, and the ability to wake without a fight are more revealing than whether the child's sleep duration matches the midpoint of a population guideline.

The Myth, and the Truth

The myth: There is one correct number of hours you should sleep, determined by your age, and if you hit that number you're fine.

The more accurate version: Sleep duration recommendations are evidence-based starting points — useful for orienting yourself, useless as verdicts. The real measure of whether you're getting enough sleep is whether you wake able to meet the demands of your day without fighting your own alertness. That number is different for different people, and it can shift across your lifespan. The guidelines tell you where to look. Only your own patterns can tell you what you've found.
