Sleep Hygiene and Supplements: What Actually Happens When You Take Something to Sleep

May 31, 2026 4 min read By William Novak

You are lying in bed at 11:47 PM. Your phone says 11:47. You have read the same paragraph four times. Tomorrow will be worse if you don't fall asleep soon. So you get up, walk to the bathroom cabinet, and take something.

This scene is played out millions of nights a year. The global sleep supplements market was valued at roughly $30 billion in 2023, which means a lot of people are doing exactly this — reaching for a pill, a gummy, a powder stirred into warm milk. Most of them have never been told what actually happens after that substance lands in their stomach. And a few of them might sleep better if they knew.

Sleep hygiene is the term for the environmental and behavioral groundwork that makes sleep possible. Supplements sit on top of that groundwork. If the foundation is not solid, no additive will fix it. But understanding the path a supplement takes through your body — from gut to bloodstream to brain — makes it easier to know whether any given compound is worth trying at all.

The mechanism matters more than the marketing.

The Gut: Where It Starts

That melatonin gummy or magnesium capsule dissolves in the stomach and small intestine. The speed of absorption depends on what else is there. Food slows it down, which is why most supplement labels say "take on an empty stomach" if they want a fast effect, or "with food" if the compound can cause nausea.

Melatonin is absorbed quickly — peak blood levels usually hit within 30 to 60 minutes. But the form matters. A 2017 study by Gooneratne et al. (Journal of Pineal Research) compared immediate-release and prolonged-release melatonin and found that the immediate form produced a sharper spike in blood levels but didn't sustain sleep as well. The prolonged form blunted the spike but improved sleep maintenance. Most over-the-counter melatonin is immediate-release. Most people do not know this.

Magnesium — the glycinate form, specifically, which has the best evidence for sleep — is absorbed more slowly and less completely. The body holds on to what it needs and excretes the rest. This is one reason magnesium is generally safe: excess is rarely an issue. But it also means that a single dose before bed may not produce a noticeable effect if your magnesium status is already adequate.

The Liver: The Gate Nobody Talks About

After absorption, every orally taken compound passes through the liver before it reaches general circulation. This is called first-pass metabolism, and it is the reason that oral doses are much higher than intravenous doses of the same drug. The liver strips away a portion of the active compound before it ever reaches the brain.

Melatonin's first-pass metabolism is substantial. Oral bioavailability is estimated at around 15 percent. The other 85 percent is broken down in the liver before it can do anything. This is why the standard doses sold in stores — 5 mg, 10 mg — are vastly higher than what the body actually needs. The physiological dose produced by the pineal gland at night is about 0.3 mg. Anything above 1 mg is, for most people, more than necessary, and some evidence suggests that higher doses can produce grogginess the next morning because the compound lingers in the bloodstream.

Magnesium glycinate bypasses this problem differently — it is not primarily a brain-targeted compound. Its sleep effects come from relaxing skeletal muscle and modulating the nervous system's stress response, not from directly altering sleep-wake signaling.

The Brain: Where It Either Works or Doesn't

Once a supplement crosses the blood-brain barrier — and not all do — it interacts with specific receptor systems.

Melatonin binds to MT1 and MT2 receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain's master clock. This signals that it is dark and that the body should prepare for sleep. The effect is modest. A well-cited meta-analysis by Ferracioli-Oda et al. (2013, Sleep Medicine Reviews, 19 studies, ~1,683 participants) found that melatonin reduced the time to fall asleep by an average of about seven minutes. Not an hour. Not twenty minutes. Seven. For some individuals it helped more, for some less. The average hides a lot of variation.

L-theanine, an amino acid found in green tea, works through a different route. It increases GABA, glycine, and serotonin levels while lowering glutamate. The effect is more calming than sedating — think of it as reducing the "alertness noise" rather than forcing the sleep signal. The evidence base is thinner than melatonin's, and the largest trials have been done by companies with a commercial interest in the results.

Magnesium's sleep effects likely come through the NMDA receptor and GABA system. Magnesium is a natural NMDA receptor antagonist — it blocks the excitatory signal that keeps you awake. The magnesium glycinate form also brings glycine, which is itself an inhibitory neurotransmitter. Some people feel a difference. Many do not.

The Honest Rule of Thumb

If your sleep environment is dark, cool (18–20°C is the range from the National Sleep Foundation's temperature guidelines), and quiet; if your bedtime is within an hour of the same time most nights; and if you are not staring at screens for the thirty minutes before you try to sleep — then a supplement might shave a few minutes off how long it takes to drift off. If those conditions are not met, the supplement is unlikely to do much.

If you want to try one, choose a single compound. Keep the dose low — melatonin at 0.3 to 1 mg, not 10. Take it consistently for ten nights and note whether anything changes. If nothing changes, that is information. Most people respond modestly or not at all.

Try This Instead of Buying Something

Pick one bedtime this week — it can be any night — and commit to the exact same time. Set an alarm for it, same as a morning alarm. Then, an hour before that time, dim the lights. Not just the phone; dim the actual room lights. Do that for one night and see whether you fall asleep faster than you did the week before.

The evidence for a consistent bedtime and reduced evening light exposure dwarfs the evidence for every sleep supplement combined. Start there. If that moves the needle a few minutes, you will have accomplished what most supplements promise and few deliver. If it does not, a doctor is a better next step than a different bottle.
