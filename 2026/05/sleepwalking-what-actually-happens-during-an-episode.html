Sleepwalking: What Actually Happens During an Episode

May 29, 2026 5 min read By Eleanor Chambers

I spent one night sleeping on the floor of my own living room, a cup of water balanced on my chest, because I wanted to know if I would knock it over.

This is not a normal thing to do. I had a reason. My partner had recently told me that I sometimes sit up in bed, stare at the wall for a few seconds, and lie back down. She said I looked awake. I did not remember any of it. The cup-on-chest test was my crude attempt to measure whether I was capable of complex movement during these episodes. I woke up dry. But I also had no idea whether I had stayed still all night or had simply replaced the cup in my sleep.

That unsettled feeling — not knowing what your own body is doing while you are technically asleep — is the core experience of sleepwalking, and of the larger family of NREM parasomnias it belongs to.

What sleepwalking actually is

Sleepwalking, formally known as somnambulism, is not a half-awake state. It originates during the deepest phase of non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, usually in stage N3, also called slow-wave sleep. During an episode, the brain is caught between sleep and wakefulness in a specific way: the parts responsible for complex motor behavior come online, while the parts responsible for conscious awareness and memory encoding remain offline.

That is why a person can walk, unlock a door, rearrange objects, or even drive a car, and have zero recollection of it. A key symptom — and the one that reliably distinguishes sleepwalking from other conditions — is that the person virtually never remembers the episode.

Episodes typically last between 30 seconds and 30 minutes. They almost always occur in the first third of the night, when slow-wave sleep is most abundant. They are not dreams. People who are woken during a sleepwalking episode (and it is worth noting that most experts advise against jarring awakenings — gentle guidance back to bed is safer) do not report dream-like content. They report confusion, often for several minutes.

How common is it? The numbers are narrower than you think

The common citation is that about 5% of children and 1.5% of adults experience sleepwalking. That is broadly accurate, but the most careful recent estimate comes from a 2016 meta-analysis by Stallman and Bolland, published in Sleep Medicine Reviews, which pooled data from 51 separate studies. Their prevalence figure for adults was 1.5% to 2.8% for at least one episode in the previous year. For children, the range was wider — 5% to 12% depending on how the study defined an episode.

The decline with age is real. Most children outgrow sleepwalking as their nervous system matures and the proportion of slow-wave sleep decreases. Puberty resolves it in roughly two-thirds of cases. For the adults who continue to sleepwalk, episodes often become less frequent with time, though they may re-emerge during periods of high stress or sleep deprivation.

Should you be worried about safety?

This is the question every sleepwalker or concerned partner arrives at. The answer is more measured than most people expect.

Serious injury during sleepwalking is rare. The person is not acting out a dream and is not usually violent — the behaviors are typically purposeless or habitual: walking to a door, opening a cabinet, rearranging items. The real danger is environmental: a staircase, a window, a glass door, leaving the house.

Harm reduction is the first-line intervention for most cases. What that looks like in practice:

  • Remove sharp objects and tripping hazards from the bedroom and hallway.
  • Lock windows and doors, and keep keys out of reach if the person has ever left the house during an episode.
  • Consider a motion-sensor light in the hallway to reduce disorientation if the person does get up.
  • Door alarms are not overkill for adults who have a history of leaving the house.

These measures are not admission that the problem is severe. They are the same kind of reasonable precaution you take for a toddler who might wander in the night — and for the same reason: the person cannot reliably keep themselves safe during the episode.

What causes an episode?

The short answer is that sleepwalking appears when two conditions converge: a genetic predisposition and an acute trigger that deepens or fragments slow-wave sleep.

Genetics

Heritability is substantial. A large population-based twin study by Hublin et al. (1997), published in Neurology, found a concordance rate of 55% for monozygotic twins versus 35% for dizygotic twins. More recent work by a French-Canadian research group (Lecendreux et al., 2003, Neurology) suggested that a specific variant of the HLA-DQB1 gene is more common among frequent sleepwalkers — though this finding has not been replicated consistently enough to be clinically useful. The consensus is that the genetic component is real but polygenic, meaning many small contributions rather than a single sleepwalking gene.

Triggers

Sleep deprivation is the most reliable trigger. A night of poor sleep increases slow-wave pressure — the brain's drive to recover deep sleep — and that rebound can make the transition between sleep stages unstable. Fever, alcohol (which fragments sleep in the second half of the night), and certain medications (particularly zolpidem and other Z-drugs) can do the same.

Sleep-disordered breathing is an under-recognized contributor, especially in adults. Obstructive sleep apnea causes repeated micro-arousals from deep sleep, and each arousal is a chance for a confusional episode. When OSA is treated with CPAP, sleepwalking frequency often drops. If an adult develops sleepwalking for the first time in middle age, sleep apnea is a reasonable first thing to investigate.

Does treatment work?

For children whose episodes are not dangerous, the best intervention is usually reassurance and patience. The data on active treatment is thinner than the confidence with which it is sometimes stated. Scheduled awakening — waking the child about 30 minutes before an episode typically occurs, for a period of weeks — has some support from small case series (Frank et al., 1968, being the most cited, which is itself a sign of thin evidence). It may work by disrupting the sleep architecture that allows episodes to start.

For adults, the evidence base is limited to case reports and small trials. Clonazepam, a benzodiazepine that suppresses slow-wave sleep, is the most commonly prescribed medication. A 2019 review in Current Treatment Options in Neurology noted that low-dose clonazepam (0.5 to 2 mg at bedtime) reduces episode frequency in many adults, but the drug carries risks of tolerance, morning sedation, and dependency. It is not a first-line treatment for mild cases.

Cognitive-behavioral approaches — addressing sleep hygiene, treating comorbid insomnia, reducing alcohol — have face validity but almost no controlled data.

The unsettled question

The cup on my chest experiment answered nothing. I still do not know what my body does during those brief nighttime moments my partner describes. That is the strange epistemic position sleepwalking puts you in: the person who experiences it has the least access to it.

What I do know is that the unsettled feeling is not the same as danger. Most sleepwalkers do not hurt themselves. Most children outgrow it. And the most useful thing a person can do is not to try to fix the sleepwalking directly, but to fix the things that make it more likely — sleep schedule, sleep apnea, stress — and to make the environment safe enough that an episode, when it happens, is just a strange story in the morning.

One question remains genuinely open: whether sleepwalkers have any conscious experience during an episode that they simply cannot access later, or whether the experience is truly absent. The phenomenology of NREM parasomnias is almost unstudied. We do not know what it feels like, or if it feels like anything at all. And that may be the most honest thing we can say about it.
