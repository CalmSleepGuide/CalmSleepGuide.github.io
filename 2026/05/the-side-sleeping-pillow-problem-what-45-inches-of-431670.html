The Side Sleeping Pillow Problem: What 4.5 Inches of Loft Actually Means

May 24, 2026 5 min read By Hannah Mercer

In a 2021 study published in the Journal of Physical Therapy Science, researchers measured what happens to the cervical spine when side sleepers use pillows of different heights. The number that came out of it — 4.5 inches — has since been copy-pasted across pillow guides, mattress reviews, and sleep blogs as though it were a universal prescription.

It is not. But it is worth understanding, because the logic behind that number is the same logic that solves neck pain and shoulder soreness for most side sleepers. The trick is knowing when to follow it and when to ignore it.

What the Study Actually Measured

The researchers asked 24 healthy adults to lie on their side while a radiograph captured the alignment of their cervical vertebrae. Each participant tested three pillow heights: 3.1 inches, 4.5 inches, and 5.9 inches. The finding was that the 4.5-inch pillow produced a cervical angle closest to neutral — meaning the spine from C1 to C7 formed a roughly straight line, as it does when standing upright with good posture.

What this measures: lateral cervical alignment, nothing else. The pillow height fills the gap between the ear and the far shoulder, keeping the head from tilting up toward the ceiling or down toward the mattress. That is the mechanical problem a pillow solves for a side sleeper, and 4.5 inches was the best fit for the average participant.

But "average participant" in this study meant young, healthy adults with no pre-existing cervical pathology and — critically — no significant variation in shoulder width. The researchers controlled for it by screening participants. Real side sleepers do not come pre-screened.

What 4.5 Inches Does Not Measure

The study tells you the ideal loft for a population whose shoulders are roughly the same width. It does not tell you much about the following, all of which determine whether that loft actually works for you in practice.

Shoulder width. A broader-shouldered side sleeper needs more loft to fill the larger gap between ear and mattress. A narrower-shouldered sleeper needs less. Four-point-five inches is a starting point, not a destination. If you wake up with the side of your neck compressed — as though the pillow is pushing your head up — you likely need lower loft. If you wake up with your top shoulder rolled forward and your bottom arm numb, you likely need higher loft.

Shoulder sink. When you lie on your side, your shoulder does not sit flat against the mattress. It compresses into it. How much depends on your weight, your mattress firmness, and the construction of the pillow itself. A 4.5-inch memory foam pillow with high density may behave like a 5-inch pillow because the foam resists compression. A 4.5-inch shredded latex pillow may behave like a 3.5-inch pillow because the fill shifts and compresses easily.

Sleeping posture variation. No one stays perfectly on their side all night. Most side sleepers drift into a semi-fetal position, a near-stomach position, or a back-lying position at some point. A pillow that works beautifully at 90 degrees of lateral rotation may feel wrong at 120 degrees. This is why adjustable-fill pillows exist — not as a marketing gimmick, but because human sleep is not static.

Pain location. If the study had measured muscle activation or nerve compression, it would have told a more useful story. A pillow can produce perfect cervical alignment on an X-ray while still compressing the brachial plexus enough to wake you with a numb arm. It can keep the neck straight but fail to support the thoracic spine, causing the upper back to bear load it should not. The 4.5-inch figure is about bones, not nerves or soft tissue.

A Better Framework Than Loft Alone

After testing two dozen pillows over the past year — in my own bed, with my own sleep data, and with the kind of obsessive note-taking that usually precedes a return to the manufacturer — I have settled on three variables that matter more than a single loft measurement.

Compression resistance

A pillow's loft when you place it on the bed is not its loft when your head rests on it. The relevant measurement is compressed loft — the height of the pillow under load. A dense latex pillow with a 5-inch uncompressed loft may compress to 3.5 inches under a 12-pound head. A soft down pillow with a 5-inch uncompressed loft may compress to 2 inches under the same weight.

For side sleeping, you need the compressed loft to equal the distance from your ear to your far shoulder, measured from the mattress surface while you lie down. The only way to know this is to test it in your sleeping position. Online loft charts are rough estimates at best.

Shoulder cutout or give

A pillow that maintains its shape at the shoulder-contact point will push the shoulder forward, rotating the torso and straining the upper trapezius. The best pressure-relieving pillows for side sleepers either have a contoured shoulder cutout (common in cervical pillows) or use a fill material that compresses easily at the edges (shredded latex or down alternative).

I measured this by lying on each pillow and checking whether my bottom shoulder could settle into a neutral position — not rolled forward, not pinned beneath me — while my head remained centered on the pillow surface. Pillows that failed this test consistently produced soreness in the upper back by morning.

Edge-to-edge consistency

Many pillows are denser in the center than at the edges. This is fine for back sleepers, who tend to stay centered. For side sleepers, who shift position and rest near the pillow's perimeter, it creates a loft drop-off at exactly the point where the neck needs support.

The best pillow I tested had uniform fill density from edge to edge, so that when I rolled from one side to the other — as I do roughly four times per night, according to my sleep tracker — the support surface did not change. The worst had a visible depression at the edges after two weeks of use.

Who This Framework Is For

It is for side sleepers who have bought three different pillows based on loft charts and still wake up with neck pain. It is for anyone who has tried a 4.5-inch pillow, found it close but not quite right, and assumed the problem was them. It is not for people whose neck pain stems from a diagnosed condition such as cervical radiculopathy or osteoarthritis — those cases need a physical therapist or a sports medicine specialist, not a pillow guide.

It is also not for people who sleep exclusively on their back or stomach. Back sleepers need a lower loft (typically 3 to 4 inches uncompressed, depending on neck curvature) and a different fill profile. Stomach sleepers often do best with almost no loft at all — a thin, soft pillow that keeps the neck from extending backward. The 4.5-inch figure applies only to side sleeping, and only to the specific mechanical problem of lateral cervical alignment.

What I Actually Sleep On

After six months of rotating pillows, keeping a sleep log, and making myself insufferable at dinner parties, I use a shredded latex pillow with an adjustable zipper panel. I keep the fill at roughly 80 percent of the pillow's total capacity — enough to give me about 5 inches of uncompressed loft, which compresses to roughly 4 inches under my head. I am average-shouldered, sleep on a medium-firm mattress, and tend to run warm. The latex stays cooler than memory foam, and the adjustability lets me dial the loft up or down when my shoulders feel tighter or when I switch between my side and back during a given night.

That 4.5-inch study number was useful. It gave me a target to test against. But the pillow that works is the one whose compressed loft matches my shoulder width, whose fill gives at the contact edge, and whose support is the same at 2 a.m. as it was at 11 p.m. No study can tell you that number. You have to lie down and find it.
