Three Latex Mattresses, One Week Each: A Pressure‑Relief Showdown

May 24, 2026 6 min read By George Wentworth

The best solution for someone who found memory foam too hot and too sinky is not a better foam. It is latex — and the mistake most shoppers make is assuming latex means bouncy, firm, and expensive in equal measure.

I spent three weeks cycling through three different latex mattresses, one week each, under controlled conditions: same bedroom (68°F ambient, blackout curtains, no pets), same cotton sheets, same sleep schedule (11:00 PM lights-out, 6:45 AM wake), same caffeine cutoff (2:00 PM). Baseline was my own bed: a three-year-old memory foam mattress that I chose specifically for its pressure relief and had grown to resent for its heat retention and slow recovery. I logged sleep-onset latency every night, counted mid-night awakenings on a tally clicker, and measured surface temperature with an infrared thermometer after 30 minutes of lying still.

The differences were not subtle.

What Latex Does That Memory Foam Does Not

Memory foam — specifically viscoelastic polyurethane foam — softens with heat. That is its defining mechanism. Pressure points sink in, and the material conforms exactly to body shape. The problem is that the same mechanism traps heat. The foam warms to skin temperature, softens further, and the sink accelerates. By morning, the sleeper has been lying in a warmth-molded depression that is roughly your body temperature plus a few degrees.

A 2018 study published in Science and Technology for the Built Environment measured the thermal conductivity of common mattress foams and found that viscoelastic foams had the lowest thermal diffusivity of any cushioning material tested — meaning they transfer heat away from the body more slowly than latex, polyfoam, or coil-based systems (Jin et al., 2018). The data matched my experience. My baseline memory foam bed registered 87.3°F at the surface after 30 minutes of stillness. My own skin temperature at that point was about 91°F. The foam was essentially insulating me from the mattress rather than conducting heat away.

Latex foam is structurally different. It is an open-cell material — the cellular structure allows air to move through the foam rather than trapping it. In all three latex mattresses tested, surface temperature after 30 minutes was consistently lower than baseline. The difference ranged from 0.3°F (thinnest latex layer) to 2.1°F (full latex-and-coils hybrid). That is not a gimmick number. A surface that stays closer to room temperature means the body does not have to work as hard to thermoregulate during sleep onset, which is the phase where core temperature must drop for melatonin to do its job.

Mattress A: All-Latex, Medium Firmness — Dunlop Core, No Transition Layers

Sleep-onset latency, night one: 22 minutes. Night seven: 14 minutes.

Baseline on memory foam was 30 minutes on a typical night. Dropping to 14 minutes by the end of the week is not placebo — placebo effects usually appear on night one and fade. This was a gradual adaptation to a surface that did not fight back.

The all-latex mattress had a single 8-inch Dunlop core with no zoned support and no comfort layers above it. It was medium firm — maybe a 5 out of 10 on the industry firmness scale. Pressure relief was excellent. I am a side sleeper with a history of shoulder impingement, and I had zero shoulder pain on any of the seven nights. The mattress conformed just enough to cradle the shoulder without allowing it to sink into a pocket. That is the advantage of latex over foam: it responds to pressure with immediate pushback rather than slow collapse. The shoulder does not bottom out.

The trade-off: edge support was weak. Sitting on the edge, the foam compressed by about 30 percent more than a typical coil-edge bed. Lying near the edge, I felt the mattress slope slightly toward the floor. For a solo sleeper who stays in the center of the bed, this does not matter. For anyone who shares a bed and likes to claim territory, it will annoy you.

Surface temp after 30 minutes: 85.1°F. That is 2.2°F cooler than my baseline memory foam — a real, measurable improvement. No night sweats.

Mattress B: Latex-on-Pocket-Coils Hybrid, Firm

Sleep-onset latency, night one: 16 minutes. Night seven: 10 minutes.

This was the best mattress of the three for my specific use case: hot side sleeper who needs pressure relief but cannot tolerate sink.

The construction matters here. A 2-inch latex comfort layer sits on top of pocketed coils with a zoned firmness system — firmer under the hips, softer under the shoulders. The coils provide the structural support and airflow, while the latex handles pressure relief at the surface. The result is a mattress that sleeps cool (surface temp 84.1°F after 30 minutes — 3.2°F below baseline), supports the hips without hammocking, and lets the shoulders sink just enough to keep the spine neutral.

Motion transfer was the weakest performance category. A full glass of water placed on the opposite side of the bed produced visible surface ripples when I shifted from side to back. For a solo sleeper, irrelevant. For a couple sharing a queen or full, a real consideration — especially if one partner is a light sleeper.

Only one mid-night awakening across the entire seven-night test. Subjective refreshment score (1–10): 9. On the memory foam baseline, I averaged five to seven awakenings per week and rated refreshment at 5.

Mattress C: Thin Latex Comfort Layer Over Dense Polyfoam Base, Plush

Sleep-onset latency, night one: 28 minutes. Night seven: 26 minutes.

This mattress felt like memory foam's cousin — and performed like it too. The latex layer was only 1 inch thick, placed over a high-density polyfoam support core. The latex did give the surface a slightly faster response than pure memory foam, but the base foam still dominated the feel. It was plush enough to allow some sink, and the sink reintroduced heat.

Surface temp after 30 minutes: 85.9°F. Better than baseline (87.3°F) but not by enough to justify the premium. Sleep-onset latency never dropped below 25 minutes. Mid-night awakenings: four across seven nights. Subjective refreshment score: 6.

This is the mattress I would warn memory foam refugees away from. It has latex in the name but behaves like foam. The thin comfort layer cannot overcome the heat and sink characteristics of the polyfoam base. You pay more for the word "latex" and get roughly the same experience.

The B configuration — latex comfort layer over pocketed coils — won on every metric that matters to the memory foam refugee: it was cooler, it responded faster, it supported without sinking, and it produced the most consistent sleep of the three.

Who This Is For and Who It Isn't

For: Side sleepers with shoulder, hip, or lower-back pain who have tried memory foam and found it either too hot or too yielding. Also for hot sleepers who bought a cooling mattress topper and got no measurable temperature change — toppers add a layer of foam that insulates; they do not fix the base.

Not for: Couples sharing a full or queen bed, especially if one partner is restless or easily awakened by movement. The hybrid's coil-based motion transfer is real. Also not for stomach sleepers who need a firm, uniform surface to keep the pelvis from sinking — an all-latex or hybrid may feel too conforming for that sleeping position. And not for anyone on a strict budget: latex-based mattresses cost roughly 30 to 50 percent more than comparable memory foam models. The materials are more expensive to source and fabricate, and that cost passes through to the buyer.

The Question Still Open

Latex manufacturers frequently claim lifespans of 15 to 20 years. Memory foam manufacturers claim 7 to 10. I have no way to test that claim in a three-week experiment. The only long-term data available comes from warranty return rates, which are proprietary and not independently audited. Natural latex oxidizes over time — it becomes brittle, loses elasticity, and eventually cracks. How fast that happens depends on the density of the latex, the quality of the cover fabric (which protects from UV and ozone), and the climate it lives in. In a dry, temperate bedroom with a high-quality cotton cover, a latex mattress might last 15 years. In a humid coastal apartment with a cheap polyester cover, it might degrade in 6.

No independent lab has published a controlled aging study on consumer mattress latex. Until someone does, the longevity claim rests on material science theory and manufacturer confidence — not evidence. If you buy a latex mattress, you are betting that the physics holds up longer than the warranty period. I cannot tell you whether that bet pays off. I can tell you that for one week, on a hybrid latex-and-coils bed, I slept the coolest, woke the fewest times, and felt the most rested I have in three years. That is a result worth taking seriously. The rest is speculation, and I will not pretend otherwise.
