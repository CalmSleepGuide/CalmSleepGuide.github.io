Wake-Up Lights Won't Fix Your Sleep — But They Definitely Fix Your Mornings

May 28, 2026 5 min read By Daniel Coleridge

The common claim is that a wake-up light will "fix your circadian rhythm" and suddenly you'll be a morning person. That's mostly marketing. What a wake-up light can do — and this is worth knowing — is change the single most stressful moment of your day from a jolt to a slide.

I spent a month testing three approaches to waking up: a sunrise-simulating alarm clock, a smart bulb programmed to fade on over 30 minutes, and a baseline week of my usual phone alarm. I tracked sleep-onset latency, wake time consistency, and — most important — that first 60 seconds after opening my eyes.

The verdict: None of them made me fall asleep faster. All three light-based approaches made waking up measurably less awful. That distinction matters more than most reviews admit.

How I tested

Every night for 28 days: same bedtime (10:45 PM ± 15 minutes), same bedroom temperature (68°F), same blackout curtains. Week one was my baseline — phone alarm, no light simulation. Week two: a dedicated sunrise alarm clock with a 30-minute fade-in. Week three: a smart tunable-white bulb on a gradual sunrise routine. Week four: a 15-minute fade-in on the same smart bulb.

I logged wake-up heart rate (estimated via finger-on-camera pulse), subjective grogginess on a 1–10 scale (taken 90 seconds after waking), and mid-night disruptions.

What changed — and what didn't

Sleep-onset latency stayed flat

Across all four weeks, my time to fall asleep averaged 18 minutes — with no meaningful difference between the light-free and light-assisted conditions. This surprised me. I'd assumed a sunset simulation feature (which both light-based devices offered) would cue drowsiness. It didn't. Or at least, it didn't measurably reduce how long my brain took to shut down.

A 2022 systematic review in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that evening light therapy can improve sleep onset for people with delayed sleep phase disorder, but for neurotypical sleepers the evidence is thin. My experience matches the data: if you buy a sunrise alarm specifically to fall asleep faster, you'll probably be disappointed.

Wake-up heart rate dropped — significantly

My baseline wake-up heart rate averaged 78 bpm — noticeably elevated compared to my resting rate of 58 bpm. The phone alarm (a standard iOS "Radar" tone) was the likely culprit. Week two, with the sunrise alarm's 30-minute light fade plus a gentle bird-song audio cue at the end, my wake-up heart rate averaged 63 bpm. Week three (smart bulb) was similar at 64 bpm.

That's a 15–18 bpm drop in the first minute of waking. Subjectively, it's the difference between feeling ambushed and feeling like you just happened to be awake when the alarm went off.

Sample of one, caveat applied. But a 2020 study from the University of Oxford found that abrupt auditory alarms trigger a 10–20 bpm heart rate spike in 83% of participants. The light-based alternative avoids the spike by making the auditory element — if you use one — much quieter and less percussive.

Subjective grogginess improved, but not how I expected

On my 1–10 scale (1 = ready to run, 10 = need another hour), baseline mornings averaged 6.5. Light-assisted mornings averaged 4.2. That's a real improvement.

But the improvement wasn't about feeling "more awake." It was about feeling less assaulted. The grogginess still took 15–20 minutes to lift (same as baseline), but the experience of those first minutes was calmer. I wasn't fighting the urge to throw my phone across the room. I was just lying there, watching light spread across the ceiling, waiting for my brain to catch up.

30-minute fade vs. 15-minute fade

Week four tested whether the fade duration mattered.

Metric Baseline 30-min fade 15-min fade
Avg wake-up HR 78 bpm 63 bpm 66 bpm
Subjective grogginess (1–10) 6.5 4.2 4.5
Mid-night disruptions (avg/night) 0.7 0.6 0.6
Actually woke before alarm 2 of 7 days 4 of 7 days 3 of 7 days

The 15-minute fade was almost as effective as the 30-minute version. The gap isn't big enough to declare a winner, but it suggests that the benefit plateaus — once the light has enough ramp time to avoid startling you, adding more time doesn't help proportionally.

I preferred the 30-minute version, but that's personal. Someone who wakes up at the last possible moment probably won't tolerate a half-hour pre-alarm glow.

Dedicated device vs. smart bulb

People ask whether a dedicated sunrise alarm clock is worth the markup compared to a $25 smart bulb with a sunrise routine programmed in. My answer: depends on what you value.

Dedicated device advantages: It has its own speaker (so no phone needed), the light quality is warmer and more consistent, and the integration is seamless — no app configuring, no routine troubleshooting. Mine also included a sunset simulation mode that dimmed over 30 minutes, which I tried but didn't find helpful for sleep onset (see above).

Smart bulb advantages: Cheaper, and if you already have a smart home hub, it integrates with your existing systems. I could trigger mine from a bedside button, voice, or motion sensor. The flexibility is real — but the setup friction is also real. I spent 20 minutes getting the routine right, and it broke twice during the month when a firmware update reset the bulb's schedule.

The dedicated device won on reliability. The smart bulb won on price and integration. Neither won on sleep quality, because they weren't really competing on that metric.

Who this is for (and who it isn't)

This is for: - People who hate their alarm clock but don't know why — it's probably the heart rate spike - Light sleepers who wake to any noise and want the auditory alarm to be as quiet as possible (with light doing most of the waking work) - Parents of young children who respond better to a gradually brightening room than a sudden sound — several readers have reported toddlers who sleep through gentle light alarms but wake happy - Tech-inclined sleepers who already have smart bulbs and want one less screen interaction in the morning

This isn't for: - People who genuinely can't get out of bed without a loud, repeated alarm — the gentle approach will probably let you sleep through it - Anyone expecting a cure for chronic insomnia or delayed sleep phase — these are tools for the waking transition, not for falling asleep - Heavy sleepers who need physical vibration to rouse — some of these devices have vibration settings; the light element won't help on its own

The myth and the truth

The myth: a wake-up light will fix your sleep.

The truth: it will fix your waking — which, on a 30-minute ramp, feels less like an interruption and more like the room decided to wake up with you. That's not a small difference. It's the difference between starting your day and surviving it.

For weekday mornings, that's enough.
