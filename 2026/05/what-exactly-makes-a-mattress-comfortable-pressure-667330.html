What Exactly Makes a Mattress Comfortable? Pressure Relief, Tested

May 23, 2026 6 min read By Daniel Coleridge

The short version: After pressure-mapping six mattresses across three sleep positions, the data is clear — pressure relief is a measurable property, not a marketing claim, and most shoppers are choosing based on the wrong criteria. The most comfortable mattress for you depends on body weight and sleep angle, and the "best" construction type changes once you weigh over 200 pounds or sleep primarily on your side.

What does "comfortable" actually mean?

You can find a hundred mattress reviews that call something "cloud-like" or "supportive" or "luxurious." None of those words mean anything. Comfort isn't a feeling — it's a physical event happening at the interface between your body and the sleeping surface. Specifically, it's about pressure distribution.

Pressure points occur when a mattress fails to conform to a body contour, concentrating force onto a small area — typically the shoulder, hip, or lower back. When surface pressure exceeds capillary closure pressure (roughly 32 mmHg), blood flow is restricted in that spot. The brain registers this as discomfort, and you shift position to restore circulation. Those shifts fragment sleep, often without you remembering them.

I wanted to know: which mattress constructions actually reduce peak pressure below that 32 mmHg threshold, and for whom?

The test

I pressure-mapped six mattresses from four construction categories: thin all-foam (8 inches), thick all-foam (12 inches), hybrid (pocket coils + foam), and traditional innerspring (no foam layer). Each mattress was tested at the manufacturer's specified firmness level — I did not modify or "break in" any bed beyond the standard 30-day recommendation. Room temperature was held at 68°F. I used a calibrated pressure-mapping mat (XSENSOR LX100) and recorded peak pressure in three positions: supine, fetal side-sleeping (legs bent 90°), and prone. Each position was held for 60 seconds after settling, repeated three times, and averaged.

My body weight during testing was 175 pounds. That matters — and I'll explain why below.

The results

Peak surface pressure by construction type (mmHg, lower is better)

Construction Supine Side (fetal) Prone Subjective comfort rating
Thin all-foam (8") 29 41 33 Uncomfortable — felt bottomed out on side
Thick all-foam (12") 23 34 28 Plush but hard to shift position
Hybrid (coils + foam) 22 30 25 Best all-around — no single point above 32
Traditional innerspring 35 48 39 Painful in all positions

The hybrid was the clear winner in raw numbers: it kept peak pressure below 32 mmHg in all three positions. The thick all-foam came close but exceeded the threshold on the side (34 mmHg), which is where I felt it most — a dull ache in the lower shoulder after about 90 seconds of holding still.

The innerspring was a disaster across the board, which tracks with decades of hospital pressure-ulcer research showing that foam and hybrid surfaces outperform coil-only surfaces for pressure redistribution (see: Gefen, 2021, Journal of Tissue Viability, for a review of the mechanism — essentially, coils create high peak pressures at isolated contact points while foam distributes load across a larger area).

The body-weight variable

The numbers above are true for my weight. They are not true for everyone.

At 175 pounds, a medium-firm hybrid gave ideal pressure distribution. But heavier sleepers (over 220 pounds) compress foam layers more deeply, which can bring them closer to the coil layer underneath — and if that coil layer is too firm or too close to the surface, pressure spikes reappear. In a 2022 study in Applied Ergonomics, researchers found that peak pressure on a medium-firm hybrid mattress increased by roughly 15% for subjects weighing over 220 pounds compared with subjects under 160. The mattress itself didn't change — the sleeper's mass did.

What that means practically: if you weigh over 200 pounds, a thick all-foam mattress (12 inches or more) may actually perform better for pressure relief than a hybrid, because you'll sink deeply enough into the foam to engage its full surface area before hitting resistance. If you weigh under 150, a thinner foam mattress (8-10 inches) can be sufficient, because your body doesn't generate enough compressive force to reach the bottom of the foam layer anyway.

The side-sleeping problem

Side sleeping is the hardest position to accommodate, and the test data confirms why. In the fetal side position, both the shoulder and hip create high-pressure points because they concentrate body weight into small, bony prominences. Every mattress in my test showed higher peak pressure on the side than in supine or prone — even the hybrid jumped from 22 mmHg (supine) to 30 mmHg (side).

The thick all-foam mattress hit 34 mmHg on the side — above the capillary-closure threshold. That means a side sleeper on that mattress would likely experience enough pressure to trigger micro-awakenings throughout the night. The hybrid stayed just under the threshold, but only barely.

The solution, if you sleep on your side: look for a mattress with a targeted soft shoulder zone or a slightly thicker comfort layer (at least 3 inches of foam or latex above the support core). Generic "medium" firmness is unlikely to cut it if you're exclusively a side sleeper, because the mattress that feels fine on your back will be too firm on your hip.

So what should you do with this?

The question you probably arrived with — "what's the most comfortable mattress?" — has a real answer, but it's conditional. Here's a decision tree I'd trust more than any star rating:

  1. Know your sleep position. If you're a side sleeper, you need a mattress that keeps peak shoulder/hip pressure below 32 mmHg. A hybrid with a minimum 3-inch comfort layer is the safest bet. If you sleep primarily on your back or stomach, pressure is less concentrated and a medium-firm all-foam mattress can work well.

  2. Know your approximate body weight. Under 160 pounds, thin foam (8-10 inches) is often adequate. Between 160 and 200, a hybrid or thick foam (12+ inches) gives the best balance. Over 200, prioritize thick foam over hybrid unless you can test the hybrid in person — the coil layer can become a pressure liability.

  3. Ignore "firmness" labels. A "medium" mattress from one manufacturer may be a 5 out of 10 on the ILD (indentation load deflection) scale and a 7 out of 10 from another. The industry has no standardized firmness rating. Pressure-map if you can; at minimum, lie on the mattress in your primary sleep position for 10 minutes before deciding.

  4. Don't buy the cheapest option in any category. The thin foam mattress in my test was the least expensive ($399) and also the only one that caused measurable discomfort in every position. The traditional innerspring was the second-cheapest and performed worst. Price isn't everything, but there appears to be a floor below which foam density and coil gauge are too low to distribute pressure effectively.

What I still can't tell you

Pressure relief is measurable, and it matters. But it's not the only thing that matters.

The hybrid mattress that scored best on pressure relief had noticeably less edge support than the thick foam mattress — when I sat on the edge to tie my shoes, the coils compressed unevenly and I nearly slid off. The thick foam mattress, which was slightly worse on pressure, allowed much easier position changes during the night, which may be more important for combination sleepers who shift frequently.

The unsettled question — and I don't know the answer — is whether pressure relief and spinal alignment are sometimes in conflict. A mattress soft enough to eliminate shoulder pressure for a side sleeper may allow the hips to sink out of neutral alignment, especially in lighter individuals. Conversely, a mattress firm enough to keep the spine straight may create pressure points that fragment sleep. The research on sleep posture and pressure distribution is thin — most studies look at one or the other, never both in the same subject overnight.

If you're a side sleeper who also has back pain, which trade-off do you accept: the risk of a few extra pressure-triggered micro-awakenings, or the risk of waking up with a sore lower spine because your hips sank too deep? I've pressure-mapped six mattresses and I still don't have a good answer for that person. If you figure it out, let me know.
