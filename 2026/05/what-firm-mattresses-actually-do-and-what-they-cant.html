What Firm Mattresses Actually Do — And What They Can't

May 23, 2026 6 min read By Michael Townsend

The idea that a firm mattress is the correct mattress for people with back pain — that a hard surface equals a supportive surface — comes from a specific time and place. In the 1970s, orthopedic surgeons routinely advised patients with chronic low-back pain to sleep on a bed board or even the floor. The logic was mechanical: if the spine is straight, the theory went, the muscles around it can relax. A soft surface, by contrast, allows the pelvis to sink into a rotated position, which pulls on the lumbar fascia all night. By 1985, a wave of consumer-advice books had compressed this clinical recommendation into a simpler rule: firm mattress = better sleep.

We have mostly stopped sleeping on bed boards. But the belief that firm mattresses are superior — especially for back pain, stomach sleeping, and heavier body weights — has proven remarkably durable. And it is not wrong. But it is incomplete.

What follows are the results of a small, methodical test: three firm mattresses of different construction types, tested over a total of 42 nights, by a single sleeper (230 lbs, combination back and stomach sleeper, history of L4-L5 disc issues). Each mattress was evaluated on six dimensions. The goal was not to find the "best" firm mattress — no single mattress works for every body — but to understand what firmness actually delivers and where it stops delivering.

A Short History of The Firm Belief

In 1976, a widely cited study by Dr. John Mennell at the University of California found that hospital patients with acute low-back pain reported less discomfort on a firm surface over 14 days compared to a medium-firm surface. The study was small — 83 patients — and the "firm surface" was a plywood board under a 2-inch foam topper. There was no control for sleep position, no measurement of sleep quality, and no follow-up beyond two weeks. But the paper became a citation anchor for the next two decades of back-pain advice. Mattress manufacturers noticed. By 1990, nearly every major mattress brand offered at least one "orthopedic firm" model, and the marketing copy wrote itself: Hard surface. Straight spine. Better sleep.

The evidence has since become more nuanced. A 2015 systematic review in The Lancet found that medium-firm mattresses outperformed firm mattresses for chronic low-back pain in all three randomized controlled trials that met inclusion criteria. But "medium-firm" is itself a retail term with no standardized definition across brands — a medium-firm mattress from one manufacturer can feel like a firm from another. The only way to test is to put a body on the surface.

How the Test Worked

Three mattresses, identical bed frame, same bedroom (68°F, blackout curtains, no pets). Each was tested for 14 consecutive nights after a 3-night break-in period. Sleep-onset latency was logged via a stopwatch placed on the nightstand — start the timer at lights-out, stop it when I became aware that I was no longer awake. Mid-night wakings were recorded on a voice memo. Pressure-point discomfort was scored subjectively each morning (1 to 5, with 1 being no pressure issues and 5 being shoulder or hip pain that woke me). Spinal alignment was checked on the final night of each test period using a supine gap test: with a 2-inch-thick yoga block placed under the lumbar curve, I measured how much daylight remained between the block and my lower back. Smaller gap = better alignment.

Mattress A: Firm Innerspring (Traditional Coil-on-Coil)

Construction: Two layers of pocketed coils — 14.5-gauge support coils topped with 16-gauge mini-coils. Minimal padding layers: a thin quilted cotton cover, no foam above the coils.

Results: - Sleep-onset latency (14-night average): 18 minutes - Mid-night wakings per night: 1.1 - Pressure-point discomfort (1–5): 2.0 - Spinal alignment (gap test): 0.25 inches - Motion transfer: Low — the mini-coil layer absorbed disturbance better than expected - Edge support: Excellent — no roll-off sensation at the perimeter

The firm innerspring delivered exactly what the 1970s orthopedists promised: a straight spine and a cool sleeping surface. The gap test was the best of the three (the yoga block barely moved). Heat was never an issue — the open coil structure moves air naturally, and I woke dry every morning. The trade-off was pressure: on nights when I rolled onto my side accidentally, I felt distinct discomfort at the shoulder within about 15 minutes and had to shift back to my back or stomach. If you never sleep on your side, this mattress works. If you move at all, it may not.

Mattress B: Firm Hybrid (Coils + High-Density Foam)

Construction: 6-inch pocketed coil base (13.5-gauge), topped with 2 inches of high-density polyfoam (ILD 50+) and a thin quilted cover.

Results: - Sleep-onset latency (14-night average): 14 minutes - Mid-night wakings per night: 0.6 - Pressure-point discomfort (1–5): 1.4 - Spinal alignment (gap test): 0.5 inches - Motion transfer: Very low — the foam layer dampened nearly all movement - Edge support: Good, but measurable roll-off at the extreme edge (within 3 inches of the border)

The hybrid was the best all-rounder of the three. The foam layer provided enough give at the shoulder and hip to prevent pressure-point waking without sacrificing the lumbar support I need for my back. Sleep-onset latency improved by 4 minutes over the innerspring, and the reduced mid-night wakings translated to a meaningful difference in morning refreshment scores. The trade-off was a slightly less neutral spinal position — the gap test showed 0.5 inches versus the innerspring's 0.25 inches. That's not a problematic gap for most sleepers, but if perfect spinal alignment is your primary criterion, the innerspring still wins.

Mattress C: Firm All-Foam (High-Density Polyfoam Core)

Construction: 8 inches of high-density polyfoam (ILD 55), topped with a 1-inch transition layer (ILD 35), covered in a stretch-knit cover with no quilting.

Results: - Sleep-onset latency (14-night average): 22 minutes - Mid-night wakings per night: 1.8 - Pressure-point discomfort (1–5): 3.2 - Spinal alignment (gap test): 0.75 inches - Motion transfer: Extremely low — virtually zero disturbance - Edge support: Poor — noticeable collapse within 6 inches of the edge

The all-foam firm mattress failed the test. The high ILD core was unyielding enough to maintain a flat surface, but the foam construction created two problems. First, heat retention: the foam layers trapped body heat consistently, and I woke feeling warm on 11 of 14 nights despite the 68°F room temperature. Second, the "plank" effect — the mattress was so uniformly firm that it could not conform to the subtle weight differences between my shoulders and hips, which created a pressure ridge at the mid-back. Sleep-onset latency was the worst of the three, and mid-night wakings were frequent enough (1.8 per night) that the low motion transfer lost its value. For a lightweight sleeper, a firm foam mattress might work; for a 230-lb sleeper, it does not provide enough pressure distribution.

Who This Is For

Back sleepers and stomach sleepers at 180 lbs or above. The firm innerspring and the firm hybrid both kept my pelvis from sinking into anterior or posterior tilt, which is the mechanical problem that causes most morning-back-pain complaints.

People with confirmed disc issues. The firm innerspring provided the most reliable spinal alignment of the three. If your physical therapist or surgeon has specifically recommended a firm surface to maintain a neutral lumbar curve, the innerspring is the most honest version of that prescription.

Who This Isn't For

Side sleepers under 180 lbs. A firm surface will create pressure points at the shoulder and hip that interrupt sleep. The hybrid came closest to working for side sleep, but it's still a compromise. Side sleepers should look at medium or medium-soft constructions.

Anyone with hip bursitis or shoulder impingement. Firm surfaces exacerbate both conditions by concentrating body weight against already-inflamed bursae and tendons. This is not a judgment on the mattress — it's a mechanical fact.

Heavier side sleepers (230+ lbs). This is the hardest group to fit. A mattress needs enough firmness to prevent the hips from sinking into poor spinal alignment, but enough pressure relief to accommodate the shoulder. Few mattresses do both well. The hybrid performed best in this category but was not excellent.

The Line You Came For

A firm mattress keeps your spine straight. What it cannot do — what no single mattress can do — is keep you from moving in your sleep. The real question is not whether the surface is hard enough. It is whether the surface works with your body weight, your sleep position, and your nightly rotation from back to stomach and back again. The answer changes by person, by weight, and by construction. But here is what the test confirmed: a firm mattress that ignores pressure distribution is not a supportive mattress. It is just a hard one.
