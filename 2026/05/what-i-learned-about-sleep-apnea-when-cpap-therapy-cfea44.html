What I Learned About Sleep Apnea When CPAP Therapy Failed (Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation)

May 22, 2026 6 min read By Grace Ellerton

I turned off my CPAP machine on a Tuesday night. Not out of frustration, though there had been plenty of that. I wanted to see, with my own numbers, what I was actually trading when I chose adherence over comfort.

For six weeks I had worn the mask. My AHI — apnea-hypopnea index, the number of respiratory events per hour — had dropped from 28 (moderate obstructive sleep apnea) to 4.2 (treated). By every clinical measure, CPAP therapy was working. But I was waking three to four times a night to adjust the seal. I was mouth-breathing past the humidifier. I was, on some nights, lying awake from 3:00 AM until dawn, the machine hissing beside me, wondering if this was what the rest of my life sounded like.

The night I stopped, my AHI climbed back to 26. I slept 6 hours and 12 minutes — longer than my average on CPAP — and woke with the familiar hangover of untreated apnea: dry mouth, groggy cognition, the sense that I had been running a low-grade marathon in my sleep. The data was unambiguous. And still, I did not want to put the mask back on.

How CPAP therapy works

Positive airway pressure is conceptually elegant: a bedside machine generates a column of air that splints the upper airway open during sleep, preventing the soft tissue collapse that defines obstructive sleep apnea. First described in 1981 by Colin Sullivan and colleagues at the University of Sydney, the method remains the first-line treatment for moderate to severe OSA. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends it as the initial therapy for good reason — no other intervention matches its efficacy at reducing the apnea-hypopnea index.

The problem is not the science. The problem is the person.

Adherence rates tell a sobering story. A 2019 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that approximately 34% of patients discontinue CPAP within the first year. Common barriers include mask discomfort, pressure intolerance, claustrophobia, noise, and the subjective experience of being tethered to a machine every night. These are not failures of will. They are side effects of a treatment that, for a significant minority, trades one form of sleep disruption for another.

What I measured

I logged ten nights on CPAP and ten nights off, controlling for bedtime (10:45 PM ± 15 minutes), room temperature (66°F), and evening light exposure (blue-light glasses starting at 8:00 PM). I used a wrist-worn oximeter and a bed sensor that tracks movement and respiratory rate.

Sleep-onset latency 22 min 15 min

Subjective refreshment (1-10) 5.2 4.1

The pattern is strange and instructive. On CPAP, my sleep was objectively better — my airway stayed open — but subjectively worse. I woke more often. I spent less time in deep sleep, probably because I was surface-level conscious through large portions of the night, adjusting to the pressure. Off CPAP, I slept longer and woke less, but the quality of that sleep was fundamentally degraded by respiratory events.

This is the bind that many people with OSA know well: you are choosing between two imperfect states, and neither feels like health.

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation

When I began researching alternatives, I kept encountering a device I had never heard of before my diagnosis. Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS) is a surgically implanted system that delivers mild electrical stimulation to the hypoglossal nerve, which controls tongue protrusion. During sleep, the device detects the patient's breathing pattern and stimulates the nerve at the appropriate point in the respiratory cycle, moving the tongue forward and opening the airway.

The most studied system, the Inspire device, was approved by the FDA in 2014 for adults with moderate to severe OSA who cannot tolerate CPAP. The inclusion criteria are specific: body mass index below 32, AHI between 15 and 65, and absence of complete concentric collapse at the velum — a condition that must be evaluated via drug-induced sleep endoscopy.

The STAR trial, a multicenter prospective study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2014, reported that 66% of participants achieved a 50% or greater reduction in AHI at 12 months, with a mean AHI reduction from 29.3 to 9.0. A five-year follow-up in Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery demonstrated sustained benefit: 75% of participants maintained a response at 60 months.

These numbers are not as strong as CPAP's theoretical ceiling. But they represent something CPAP cannot: adherence without nightly effort. The device is activated by a handheld remote before sleep and runs automatically. Once implanted, the patient does not have to adjust a mask, manage a humidifier, or fight a seal at 3:00 AM.

The adherence advantage of HNS is its most compelling feature. A 2020 analysis in Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine found that objective usage data from implanted devices showed median nightly use of 7.2 hours — higher than the CPAP adherence threshold of 4 hours per night used in most insurance criteria. This is not because HNS patients are more motivated. It is because the treatment does not impose a conscious burden.

Who this is for

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation is a specific tool for a specific population. You are a candidate if you have moderate to severe OSA, have tried CPAP and found it untenable, have a BMI under 32, and have no significant anatomical obstructions beyond the tongue base. You should be willing to undergo a surgical procedure with a recovery period of several weeks and accept that the device requires a battery replacement every 8-11 years.

Who it isn't

HNS is not for you if your OSA is mild and you have not tried CPAP. It is not for you if your BMI exceeds 32; weight loss should be pursued first, as obesity-driven apnea often responds better to PAP or surgical weight-loss interventions. It is not appropriate if you have central sleep apnea, a condition where the brain fails to signal breathing — nerve stimulation cannot fix a central drive problem. And it is not appropriate if drug-induced sleep endoscopy reveals complete concentric collapse at the velum, because the mechanism of tongue protrusion cannot overcome a palate that collapses inward rather than forward.

The question I still carry

I have not scheduled a consultation for HNS. I am still wearing the CPAP mask on most nights, telling myself that 5.9 hours of fractured sleep is better than 6.4 hours of untreated apnea. The data supports this. The body is less sure.

What I keep returning to is this: we have a treatment that works at the level of physiology and fails at the level of human behavior, and another that works slightly less well physiologically but requires nothing of the person at bedtime. The trade-off is profound, and the research literature has not yet answered the question that matters most to me — not whether HNS reduces AHI, but whether people who switch from CPAP to HNS report feeling better, not just sleeping better on paper.

A single small study from 2020, published in Sleep, followed 31 patients who converted from PAP to HNS. At six months, Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores improved, and 84% of participants preferred HNS. But the sample was small, the follow-up short, and the question of subjective refreshment — that slippery, personal sense that sleep has actually restored you — remains almost entirely unmeasured.

I do not know yet which treatment will be mine. But I know this: the decision is not about which device has better numbers. It is about which version of your life you can sustain. And that question, for now, only you can answer.
