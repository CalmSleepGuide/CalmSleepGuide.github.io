What Nasal Breathing Can and Can’t Do About Obstructive Sleep Apnea

May 31, 2026 11 min read By Margaret Sullivan

I spent three weeks sleeping with my mouth taped shut. Not the whole mouth — just a small strip of surgical tape across the center of my lips, the kind you see in the nasal breathing videos that have accumulated millions of views over the last few years. The claim, repeated across those videos and in a growing stack of wellness-adjacent articles, is straightforward: breathing through your nose during sleep prevents snoring, improves oxygen saturation, and can reduce or even eliminate obstructive sleep apnea.

I wanted to believe it. My own sleep study from eighteen months earlier had come back with an AHI (apnea-hypopnea index) of 14 — mild obstructive sleep apnea, technically, but enough to leave me waking with a dry throat, a headache, and the kind of daytime fatigue that makes you wonder if this is just what being adult feels like. I had not pursued treatment. The CPAP machine I tried for two nights sat in its box under the bed, and I was looking for an alternative the way anyone looks for an alternative: with hope and with selective attention to evidence.

The three-week verdict, stated plainly: nasal breathing during sleep did not fix my sleep apnea. It improved my snoring measurably and reduced my subjective sense of morning grogginess, but it did not move my AHI into normal range, and it did not eliminate the oxygen desaturations that define the condition.

This article traces a small history — how we came to believe that nasal breathing could treat a disorder of airway collapse — and reports what happened when I tried to make that belief work on an actual airway.

Where the Belief Came From

The modern nasal breathing movement traces back to a single book: Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art, by journalist James Nestor, published in 2020. Nestor's central argument is that humans have evolved away from their natural breathing pattern — nasal, slow, diaphragmatic — and toward chronic mouth breathing, with consequences that include snoring, sleep apnea, anxiety, and reduced athletic performance. The book is compelling, well-reported, and draws on real physiology. It cites legitimate research on the role of the nose in filtering, warming, and pressurizing air, and on the relationship between nasal resistance and upper airway collapsibility.

The problem is a gap between what the research shows and what the popular understanding of it claims.

Nestor's own experiment — conducted with Stanford researcher Dr. Jayakar Nayak, in which the two men had their noses completely plugged for ten days — produced dramatic results. Their sleep quality deteriorated. Their snoring became severe. One of them developed sleep apnea events where none had existed before. The takeaway, for many readers, was the inverse: if blocking the nose causes sleep apnea, then unblocking the nose and breathing through it should prevent or treat sleep apnea.

That logic assumes symmetry. It assumes that the absence of a cause is the same as a cure. And it assumes that the primary mechanism of obstructive sleep apnea is nasal resistance — that the nose is the bottleneck.

The clinical reality is more complex. Obstructive sleep apnea is a disorder of the pharyngeal airway — the space behind the tongue and soft palate. The pharynx has no bony or cartilaginous support. It is a collapsible tube held open by the tension of about twenty different muscles during wakefulness, many of which relax during sleep. When they relax enough, the airway narrows or closes entirely. The nose is upstream of this collapse point, and nasal resistance can contribute to the problem, but it is rarely the root cause.

A 2020 review in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine examined the relationship between nasal obstruction and obstructive sleep apnea and found that while nasal obstruction can worsen OSA severity — particularly by driving mouth breathing, which reduces pharyngeal airway size — treating nasal obstruction alone rarely resolves OSA. The review cited studies showing that surgical correction of nasal obstruction reduced AHI by an average of only 20 to 30 percent in most patients, and that many patients continued to have clinically significant OSA after treatment.

Twenty to thirty percent is not nothing. But it is not a cure.

What the Mouth Tape Advocates Actually Cite

When I searched the scientific literature for studies specifically testing mouth taping as a treatment for obstructive sleep apnea, I found three controlled trials.

The first, published in 2022 in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, randomized 36 patients with mild to moderate OSA to use either mouth tape or a sham device for one week. The mouth tape group showed a statistically significant reduction in AHI — from an average of 14.2 to 10.5 events per hour. Snoring frequency also decreased. But the average post-treatment AHI of 10.5 still qualified as mild sleep apnea. Only three of the eighteen patients in the treatment group saw their AHI drop below 5, which is the threshold for normal. The study concluded that mouth taping "may be a useful adjunctive therapy" — not a standalone treatment.

The second trial, from 2023 in Sleep and Breathing, looked at mouth taping combined with positional therapy (sleeping on one's side) in 28 patients. The combination reduced AHI by about 40 percent on average. But again, most patients remained in the mild to moderate range.

The third and most recent, published in early 2024, tested a specifically designed mouth-taping device (not standard surgical tape) in 42 patients with mild OSA. The results were similar: AHI dropped from about 12.3 to 8.7. Subjectively, patients reported better sleep quality and less snoring. Objectively, their oxygen desaturation index improved modestly.

Across all three trials, the pattern is consistent: mouth taping reduces snoring, improves subjective sleep quality for many users, and produces a small to moderate decrease in AHI. It does not normalize breathing for the majority of patients with even mild OSA.

The gap between the popular claim (nose breathing can fix sleep apnea) and the scientific evidence (nose breathing can mildly reduce sleep apnea severity in some patients) is not small. It is the difference between a treatment and a palliative.

My Three-Week Experiment

I am one person, and my results are not generalizable. But I logged everything systematically, and I report it here because the experience clarified something that the numbers alone could not.

Baseline (week before taping)

I recorded my sleep using a validated consumer oximeter (non-brand: a wrist-worn pulse oximeter that logs oxygen saturation and estimated AHI) for seven nights. My average bedtime was 10:45 PM. Average wake time 6:20 AM. Average sleep-onset latency: about 22 minutes. Average AHI: 14.1 (range 11.7 to 16.2). Average lowest oxygen saturation per night: 86 percent. Average snoring time per night (recorded via a separate decibel-logging app): 47 minutes. Subjective morning refreshment score (1-10, self-reported within 10 minutes of waking): 4.2.

Week one: Nasal breathing only (conscious effort, no tape)

For the first week, I simply tried to breathe through my nose as I fell asleep. I had been a habitual mouth breather at night for as long as I could remember. The first few nights required active attention. I would fall asleep breathing nasally and wake up three or four hours later with my mouth open. I would reposition, consciously close my mouth, and fall back asleep. By night four, I was waking only once or twice per night with my mouth open. My snoring time dropped to about 28 minutes per night. My AHI averaged 12.6. My subjective refreshment score: 5.1.

Week two: Surgical tape

I used standard 1-inch surgical tape, applying a small strip vertically across the center of my lips — not sealing the entire mouth, just creating a physical barrier against opening. The first night was uncomfortable. I woke twice with a sensation of air hunger, checked my oximeter, and found my saturation was actually higher than usual during those episodes — the sensation was likely anxiety about not being able to open my mouth, not actual hypoxia. By night three, I stopped noticing the tape within about fifteen minutes of lying down.

The results for week two were the best of the experiment. My AHI averaged 10.4. My snoring time dropped to 12 minutes per night. My lowest oxygen saturation averaged 88 percent. My subjective refreshment score: 6.3.

Week three: Nasal dilator strips plus tape

I added adhesive nasal dilator strips (the external kind, worn across the bridge of the nose, containing flexible spring-like bands that pull the nasal vestibule open) to test whether opening the nasal passage further would improve results. The combination was meant to address both the nasal and the oral side of the airway.

Week three was the most physically comfortable in terms of breathing — I felt like I had more air moving through my nose than at any point in my adult life. But the objective numbers barely changed from week two. My AHI averaged 10.1. Snoring time was 10 minutes. Lowest oxygen saturation: 88 percent. Subjective refreshment: 6.5.

The plateau told me something. I had reduced my nasal resistance as much as I reasonably could, and I had eliminated mouth breathing. The remaining apnea events were happening because my pharyngeal airway was still collapsing, independent of how I was breathing through my nose.

Why Nasal Breathing Isn't Enough

The pharynx is a muscular tube. When you fall asleep, the muscles that keep it open — the genioglossus, the tensor veli palatini, the hyoid muscles — lose tone. For people with sleep apnea, they lose enough tone that the airway narrows to the point of closure. Air stops moving. Oxygen drops. The brain wakes you up just enough to reopen the airway. This cycle repeats dozens or hundreds of times per night.

Nasal breathing helps at the periphery. When you breathe through your nose, the air is conditioned — warmed and humidified — which reduces airway irritation. The nasal passages also create resistance that generates negative pressure, which may help pull the pharynx open slightly during inspiration. The nasal breathing also encourages the tongue to rest against the roof of the mouth in a more stable position, rather than falling back toward the pharynx.

But these mechanisms operate at the margins of the central problem. The primary determinant of pharyngeal collapse is anatomy — the size and shape of the airway, the position of the jaw, the volume of soft tissue — and neuromuscular control during sleep. Nasal breathing cannot change your anatomy. It cannot strengthen the muscles that hold your airway open. It cannot prevent the loss of muscle tone that defines the condition.

A 2019 study in Sleep Medicine Reviews modeled the interaction between nasal resistance and pharyngeal collapsibility and found that for patients with high pharyngeal collapsibility (the most common profile in moderate to severe OSA), even complete elimination of nasal resistance reduced AHI by only about 15 percent. For patients with low pharyngeal collapsibility (a minority), the effect was larger — up to 40 percent. The takeaway: nasal breathing helps most the people who need it least.

Who This Might Be For

Mouth taping or conscious nasal breathing during sleep makes sense for three groups.

First: people who snore but do not have sleep apnea. If your bed partner reports snoring but you have no daytime fatigue, no witnessed apneas, and no risk factors (normal BMI, no retrognathia, no large neck circumference), nasal breathing may reduce or eliminate the snoring. Snoring is primarily a vibration of the soft tissues of the pharynx, and it is often louder and more disruptive during mouth breathing. Directing airflow through the nose changes the pressure dynamics and can quiet the vibration.

Second: people with mild OSA who want to reduce severity without pursuing CPAP. The data show a 20 to 30 percent reduction in AHI with consistent nasal breathing during sleep. For someone with an AHI of 12, that could mean dropping to an AHI of 8 or 9 — still mild, but closer to normal. Combined with positional therapy (sleeping on the side rather than the back), the effect may be larger.

Third: people using CPAP who have trouble with mouth leaks. Many CPAP users experience air leaking through the mouth during sleep, which reduces therapeutic pressure and causes dry mouth. Mouth taping can keep the mouth closed and maintain the pressure seal. This is the use case most frequently cited by sleep physicians who recommend the technique — not as a standalone treatment, but as a fix for a common CPAP problem.

Who This Is Not For

If you suspect you have moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea — if your bed partner tells you that you stop breathing at night, if you wake gasping, if your daytime fatigue is severe enough that you fall asleep while driving or during meetings — nasal breathing is not the answer. It will not prevent the apneas that are driven by pharyngeal collapse, and it may delay the diagnosis and treatment that you need.

The same is true if your oxygen desaturations are below 85 percent. My experiment showed that nasal breathing improved my minimum saturation from 86 to 88 percent. That is a two-point improvement at a depth that is still clinically significant. It is not safety.

The gold standard treatment for obstructive sleep apnea remains positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy. It works by physically splinting the airway open during sleep. It is not elegant. It is not something anyone wants to use. But the evidence for its effectiveness in reducing AHI, improving oxygen saturation, and reducing long-term cardiovascular risk is overwhelming. No breathing technique, no device, no supplement has come close to matching it.

What I Still Don't Know After Three Weeks

I now sleep with my mouth closed most nights, by habit. I no longer use tape. The snoring has stayed reduced, my morning refreshment has settled around 5.5 out of 10, and my AHI is probably still around 12. I have not gone back for a follow-up sleep study, so I cannot confirm the number. The improvement is real but modest.

The unsettled question, for me and for the field, is whether the modest improvement matters over the long term. If a 20 to 30 percent reduction in AHI persists for years — if someone with an AHI of 15 lives at 11 instead of 15 — does that reduce the cardiovascular risk associated with OSA? We do not know. The studies on outcomes in sleep apnea have focused on treatment thresholds: AHI above 15 is treatable, above 30 is moderate and strongly linked to adverse outcomes. We have very little data on whether moving from 15 to 11 — changing categories but not normalizing — changes life expectancy or heart health.

It is possible that any reduction in apnea burden is beneficial. It is also possible that the threshold effect is real, and that only normalization of breathing (AHI below 5) provides meaningful protection. The answer probably lies somewhere in between, and it probably depends on individual risk factors.

The science is unsettled, and I will not pretend otherwise.

What I can say, after three weeks, is that nasal breathing is not a substitute for medical treatment of obstructive sleep apnea. It is a complement at best — a small lever on a large problem. The mouth tape videos that promise otherwise are telling a partial truth. The full truth is that the nose is only part of the airway, and the part that matters most is the part you cannot reach by closing your lips.

If you snore, if you are tired, if you wake with headaches or a dry mouth: see a doctor. Get the sleep study. Find out what your AHI actually is. Then decide whether nasal breathing is something you want to add to your toolkit — not as a replacement for treatment, but as one small variable you can control while you figure out the rest.

The question that remains, and that I cannot answer from my own experiment: for how many people is that small variable enough to tip the balance from pathological to normal? The data say not many. But the data also say some. If you are among the some, you should know it from a sleep study, not from a video.
