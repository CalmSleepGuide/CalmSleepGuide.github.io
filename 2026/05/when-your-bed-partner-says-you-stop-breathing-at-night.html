When Your Bed Partner Says You Stop Breathing at Night

May 22, 2026 4 min read By Victoria Ashford

You're lying in bed, almost asleep, when your partner nudges you. "You stopped breathing," they say. "And then you gasped."

Maybe this happened once. Maybe it happens most nights. Either way, a question lodges itself: Is that normal?

It's not, and the condition it points to has a name. But the name — obstructive sleep apnea — is less important than understanding what's actually happening in your throat while you sleep, and what it means for you during the day.

The mechanism, step by step

When you fall asleep, the muscles that hold your airway open relax. This happens to everyone. For most people, the airway narrows slightly but stays open enough to move air.

For someone with obstructive sleep apnea, the relaxation goes further. The soft palate, the tongue, and the walls of the throat collapse against each other enough to block airflow entirely. Your brain notices the oxygen drop — within seconds — and wakes you up just enough to take a breath. You gulp air. You might snort or gasp. Then you fall back asleep, and the cycle repeats.

Most people with sleep apnea don't remember these awakenings. They don't feel like being woken up. They feel like sleeping through the night and waking up exhausted anyway.

So the question isn't really Am I stopping breathing at night? — you'd need a partner or a recording to know that for certain. The more useful question is: Should I look into this further?

What the research actually measured

The gold-standard test for obstructive sleep apnea is a sleep study — either in a lab or, increasingly, with a home monitoring device. A sleep study doesn't ask how you feel. It measures how many times per hour your airway closes.

This number is called the apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI. The categories are straightforward:

  • Normal: fewer than 5 events per hour
  • Mild: 5 to 15 events per hour
  • Moderate: 15 to 30 events per hour
  • Severe: more than 30 events per hour

One 2022 analysis in The Lancet Respiratory Medicine estimated that nearly one billion adults worldwide have sleep apnea, with the majority of cases undiagnosed. Another well-known study from the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort (Young et al., 1993, New England Journal of Medicine) found that 9% of women and 24% of men had at least mild sleep apnea — and those numbers have risen with obesity rates since.

But here's where the uncertainty starts. Not everyone who snorts and gasps needs treatment. And not everyone who has sleep apnea has obvious symptoms.

The honest uncertainty

The standard symptom list for sleep apnea includes loud snoring, witnessed gasping or choking, daytime sleepiness, morning headaches, and waking with a dry mouth. But these symptoms overlap with other conditions.

Loud snoring alone isn't enough. About 40% of men snore habitually, and most of them don't have sleep apnea. Meanwhile, you can have sleep apnea without snoring at all — though it's less common.

Daytime fatigue could be from sleep apnea, or it could be from poor sleep hygiene, a circadian rhythm disorder like delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, insomnia, depression, or simply not giving yourself enough time in bed. The brain fog you feel at 2 p.m. is real — but which thing is causing it isn't always obvious.

The only way to know is through a sleep study. And the decision to get one depends on how much the symptoms are affecting your life.

A small rule of thumb for tonight

If you're trying to decide whether to pursue a sleep study, here's a modest guideline: pay attention to whether your sleepiness is situational or persistent.

If you're tired only on days when you slept poorly — say, after a late night or a stressful week — that's normal physiology, not a disorder.

If you're tired most days, regardless of how many hours you spent in bed — if you fall asleep in meetings, or while reading, or during a movie you wanted to watch — that's worth investigating. The Epworth Sleepiness Scale, a short questionnaire your doctor can give you, is a reasonable starting point.

And if your partner reports that you stop breathing, don't ignore it. The data is clear that untreated moderate-to-severe sleep apnea is associated with higher rates of hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and stroke over time. The treatment — typically a CPAP machine that keeps your airway open with gentle air pressure — works well when people adjust to it.

Try this this week

Before you call your doctor, spend three nights paying attention to one thing: whether your breathing is loud and irregular enough that a partner can hear it from a few feet away. If you don't have a bed partner, you can record yourself with a voice memo app.

If the breathing is consistently loud and punctuated by silence followed by a gasp, that's the strongest single sign you can observe at home. Book an appointment with your primary care provider and say exactly that: "My partner says I stop breathing at night, and I'm tired during the day."

You don't need to self-diagnose. You just need to know when to ask the question with enough evidence that the answer matters.

The difference between a bad night's sleep and a serious medical condition isn't always clear from the inside. But the first step is just listening for what happens when you stop paying attention.
