ADHD and Sleep Disorders: What the 75% Number Actually Means

June 02, 2026 4 min read By James Prescott

Seventy-five percent.

That is the share of adults with ADHD who report clinically significant sleep problems in survey after survey. The number appears in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, in the Journal of Attention Disorders, in review papers spanning two decades. It is consistent enough that it functions almost like a diagnostic clue: if you have ADHD, the odds that you also struggle with sleep are three in four.

But that 75% is deceptive. It lumps together conditions that behave differently, respond to different treatments, and originate in different biology. The real work is not knowing the number. It is knowing which version of the number applies to you.

What the 75% actually measured

The 75% figure comes from self-report studies where adults with ADHD endorse items like “difficulty falling asleep,” “restless sleep,” or “waking up tired.” These are subjective reports from people filling out questionnaires — not objective sleep-lab data.

And that gap matters. When researchers at the University of Bergen put adults with ADHD through overnight polysomnography, they found something unexpected: many of the objective sleep measures (total sleep time, sleep efficiency, time spent in slow-wave sleep) were not dramatically different from controls. The subjective experience of sleep — how restorative it felt, how long it seemed to take to fall asleep — was significantly worse.

In other words, the 75% may be capturing real distress without capturing a single, uniform sleep pathology. People with ADHD don't just sleep badly. They perceive their sleep as bad, even when the raw architecture looks relatively intact. This perceptual gap has its own name — sleep state misperception — and it is one of the under-appreciated complications of ADHD and sleep disorders.

The specific disorders behind the number

Behind the single 75% statistic sit at least four distinct conditions, each with its own prevalence, mechanism, and treatment pathway.

Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder is the most common. Roughly 25% to 35% of adults with ADHD meet criteria for DSPS — a circadian rhythm disorder where the internal clock runs long, typically 24.5 to 25 hours instead of 24. Melatonin release is delayed by two to three hours. You are not choosing to stay up late. Your pineal gland is not signaling bedtime until midnight or later.

Restless legs syndrome appears at roughly double the rate seen in the general population. The mechanism appears to involve dopamine dysregulation — the same dopaminergic system implicated in ADHD itself. RLS creates an irresistible urge to move the legs at rest, particularly in the evening, which directly sabotages sleep onset.

Sleep-disordered breathing, including obstructive sleep apnea, is also overrepresented, possibly due to shared anatomical or neurological factors. And narcolepsy — though less common — shows a bidirectional relationship with ADHD symptoms so strong that some researchers believe the sleepiness caused by narcolepsy can provoke ADHD-like attention deficits in otherwise neurotypical individuals.

None of these conditions responds to “sleep hygiene” in the same way. A wind-down routine will not fix a circadian phase delay. Cutting caffeine will not resolve RLS. Knowing which disorder you are dealing with is not optional.

What the 75% still doesn't capture

The number also misses the circular relationship between ADHD symptoms and poor sleep. Poor sleep impairs executive function, working memory, and emotional regulation — the exact domains already taxed by ADHD. The result is a feedback loop where sleep problems worsen ADHD symptoms, which then worsen sleep. Studies that control for this bidirectional effect are rare, and the 75% statistic cannot untangle cause from consequence.

It also fails to capture the effect of ADHD medications. Stimulants taken during the day can suppress appetite and delay evening hunger cues, which can interfere with the metabolic signals that help regulate sleep-wake timing. Evening rebound effects — when the day's medication wears off — can produce a sudden surge of hyperactivity and racing thoughts right when the body should be winding down. And some non-stimulant medications (clonidine, guanfacine) are actually prescribed for sleep, which means the treatment itself becomes a confound in any simple statistic.

What this means for you

If you have ADHD and sleep poorly, the most useful thing you can do is push past the generic sleep-hygiene advice and ask a specific question: which sleep disorder do I have?

Some sleep specialists now screen for DSPS as a matter of routine in their ADHD patients. A simple sleep diary kept for two weeks — logging bedtime, wake time, and subjective alertness — can reveal a phase delay pattern that a generic “go to bed earlier” recommendation would miss. If RLS is suspected, a serum ferritin test is standard: levels below 50 to 75 mcg/L are treatable with iron supplementation.

Behavioral interventions that target the specific disorder — timed morning light therapy for DSPS, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia adapted for ADHD — have better evidence than generic sleep hygiene. The research here is still developing, but it is more targeted than it was even five years ago.

A single number told you that your sleep problem was real. The rest is about naming it.

The line worth screenshotting

Your sleep problem is not a character flaw, a hygiene failure, or a fixed part of having ADHD. It is a named condition with a known mechanism and a treatment path that starts with identifying which one it is.
