At-Home Sleep Study vs In-Lab: What the Test Doesn't Tell You

June 01, 2026 5 min read By Amelia Rutherford

The easier test is often the harder one to learn from.

An at-home sleep study fits in a shoebox. You strap it on before bed, press a button, and wake up to data. No wires taped to your scalp, no camera in the dark, no stranger watching you breathe from a control room down the hall. It costs less. You sleep in your own bed. It sounds like a clear win.

But the simpler test gives simpler answers — and for some people, the simplest answer is wrong.

If you snore heavily, wake gasping, or spend your mornings prying your tongue off the roof of your mouth, you've probably wondered whether you have sleep apnea. Maybe your partner has already said the thing: You stopped breathing last night. You want to know for sure. The question is which test will actually tell you.

Here is what the two tests measure, where each one goes blind, and how to decide which you actually need.

What a lab sleep study actually catches

A full in-lab polysomnogram (PSG) is the gold standard, and the reason is not just thoroughness — it's sequence.

In the lab, sensors track:

  • Brain waves (via electrodes on the scalp) to stage your sleep
  • Eye movements to identify REM
  • Breathing effort from belts around your chest and abdomen
  • Airflow from a nasal cannula and a thermistor at the mouth
  • Oxygen saturation from a finger probe
  • Heart rhythm from a single-lead EKG
  • Leg movements from electrodes on the shins

That list matters less for the number of channels and more for what it allows: the technician can see whether an apnea event happens during REM, whether it's caused by airway collapse or by a pause in the brain's breathing signal, and whether what looks like disordered breathing is actually a central event followed by a gasp that looks obstructive.

A lab study can distinguish obstructive sleep apnea (the airway closes) from central sleep apnea (the brain forgets to tell the diaphragm to move) and from mixed apnea (both). The treatment for each is different. CPAP helps obstruction. It does not help central apnea, and in some cases it makes it worse.

The lab also captures total sleep time precisely. If you lie in bed for seven hours but only sleep four, the machine knows — and the technician divides your apnea count by the hours you actually slept, not the hours you were in bed. That ratio (the AHI, or apnea-hypopnea index) is the number that determines whether you meet the threshold for treatment. A misestimated denominator changes the diagnosis.

What an at-home sleep study measures — and misses

A home sleep apnea test (HSAT) typically records three to four channels: airflow (nasal cannula), breathing effort (one or two belts), oxygen saturation, and sometimes heart rate. That is enough to catch a clear, straightforward case of moderate-to-severe obstructive apnea. In that scenario, the home test usually agrees with the lab.

The problems show up at the margins.

Mild OSA. A person with an AHI of 6 (mild) in the lab might blow a 3 in the home test — below the diagnostic threshold of 5 — simply because the home test cannot stage sleep. The device records everything as "time in bed" and divides by that longer denominator. The same events get averaged into a larger number of minutes, and the score drops below the line. The result is a false negative: the person is told they do not have sleep apnea when they do, and they continue sleeping poorly for months or years.

Central and complex apnea. A home test cannot distinguish a central event from an obstructive one. It sees a pause in airflow and registers it. That is all. If a significant portion of your apneas are central, the home device will report an AHI that makes you look like a straightforward candidate for CPAP, when CPAP alone may not resolve the problem.

Hypopneas. Some home tests use a different rule for scoring hypopneas (partial airway closures) than lab studies do. The older Medicare criterion requires a 4% oxygen desaturation; the newer criteria used in most labs count events with a 3% drop or an arousal visible on the EEG. A home test cannot see arousals, so it will miss any hypopnea that does not produce a clear oxygen dip. The result: more false negatives.

A 2018 meta-analysis in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine by Kapoor and colleagues pooled data from 20 studies and found that home tests have a sensitivity of roughly 80% for moderate-to-severe OSA — meaning one in five cases in that range is missed. For mild cases, the sensitivity drops further.

Who the home test works for

The home test is not a bad test. It is a test with a specific use case.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends HSAT only for patients with a high pretest probability of moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea and no major comorbid conditions. Translated: you snore loudly, your partner has witnessed apneas, you are excessively sleepy during the day, and you do not have significant heart disease, lung disease, or a neuromuscular condition. If you fit that profile and the home test comes back positive, you can reasonably start treatment.

If the home test comes back negative — or if you do not fit the high-probability profile — the AASM recommends a follow-up lab study. This is not a loophole. It is a recognition that a negative home test does not rule out sleep apnea. It rules out the kind of sleep apnea the home test was designed to catch.

Practical decision rule

Your situation Likely right test
Loud snoring, witnessed apneas, daytime sleepiness, no other major health conditions Start with HSAT
Same symptoms plus heart disease, lung disease, or history of stroke Go straight to lab
Home test came back negative but you still have symptoms Lab study
You wake up gasping but your partner says you don't snore Lab study (possible central apnea)
Your bed partner says you stop breathing and then gasp — classic obstruction HSAT is reasonable

This table is not a prescription. It is a framework for the conversation you should have with your doctor. If your physician recommends a home test and you have central apnea risk factors, ask why. If they recommend a lab and you have straightforward symptoms and good insurance, the lab is the surer path.

Honest rule of thumb

If you can get a lab study covered by insurance, do the lab study. The reason is not that the home test is useless. It is that the home test answers a narrower question, and you will not know which question you need answered until you get the result. A lab study costs more time and money upfront, but it saves you the months-long detour of a misdiagnosis.

If a home test is your only option — uninsured, geographic distance from a lab, long wait times — the test is still worth doing. Just be clear-eyed about what it can and cannot tell you. A positive result means you almost certainly have obstructive sleep apnea. A negative result means almost nothing.

The one question worth asking before you test

Ask your doctor: If my home test comes back negative, will you order a lab study?

If the answer is yes, you have a plan. If the answer is no — or if the doctor seems reluctant — that is worth pressing on. A negative home test is not a clean bill of health. It is a signal that the test was not sensitive enough for whatever is happening in your airway. The home test tells you whether you have a common problem. The lab test tells you what the problem actually is. Those are different questions, and you deserve the answer to the second one.
