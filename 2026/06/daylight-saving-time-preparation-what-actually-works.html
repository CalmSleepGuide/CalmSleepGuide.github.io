Daylight Saving Time Preparation: What Actually Works

June 05, 2026 5 min read By Nathan Briggs

You've heard the advice a dozen times: start going to bed 15 minutes earlier each night for the week before daylight saving time begins. The CDC includes it in their spring-forward guidance. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends it. It feels sensible — a gradual ramp, easing your body into the change rather than shocking it.

There is almost no good evidence that it works for most adults.

Here's what the data actually show, what's happening in your brain when the clocks change, and what you can actually do about it.

The myth of the graduated bedtime

The idea is intuitive: if you lose an hour in March, you can pre-pay that debt by borrowing small increments of sleep across the prior week. A 2017 study by Burgess and Eastman (reviewing the circadian sleep literature) found that while incremental bedtime shifts can shift the internal clock, the amount of shift per day is smaller than most people assume — about 12 to 15 minutes per day under ideal conditions. But those conditions require simultaneous control of light exposure, which almost nobody achieves while maintaining a normal work schedule.

What's more, the common advice only addresses bedtime. It says nothing about when you wake up. If you shift your bedtime earlier but your morning alarm stays fixed, you haven't shifted your circadian rhythm — you've just spent more time lying awake in the dark.

The mistake is treating DST preparation as a sleep-debt problem when it is actually a timing problem.

What the research actually measured

The best natural experiment on DST disruption comes from a 2014 study by Janssen and others at the University of Washington, which analyzed over a decade of hospital data. They found a 24% increase in acute myocardial infarction on the Monday after the spring shift, compared with surrounding Mondays. No such spike occurred after the fall shift.

Why the asymmetry? Springing forward imposes a phase advance — your internal clock is now misaligned with the sun. You are asking your body to wake up before its biological morning. Fall-back, by contrast, is a phase delay. Your clock catches up more easily, because the human circadian cycle runs slightly longer than 24 hours naturally. Delaying is what it wants to do.

The disruption, in other words, isn't about losing one hour of sleep. It's about the mismatch between your internal time and external time.

The mechanism, in the order it happens

Your circadian rhythm is governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus — a cluster of about 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus. It takes light signals from the retina and uses them to set the timing of melatonin release, core body temperature, and cortisol production.

Here is what actually happens on the Sunday of spring-forward:

You wake up at your usual clock time. Let's say 7:00 AM. But to your internal clock, it is 6:00 AM. Your melatonin hasn't fully cleared. Your core temperature hasn't begun its morning rise. You are, in a literal sense, earlier than your biology is.

The problem compounds at night. You feel sleepy at your usual evening time — say 10:00 PM — but your internal clock reads 11:00 PM. The natural rise in melatonin that should precede sleep hasn't arrived yet. So you lie awake, get less sleep, and wake up the next day even more misaligned.

This cycle can take three to seven days to resolve on its own. The variable that determines how quickly it resolves is not how much you slept the week before. It is when you get morning light.

What actually moves the needle

Morning light is the primary entraining signal for the circadian system. A 2019 study by Wright and colleagues at the University of Colorado showed that exposure to natural morning light advances the circadian clock by roughly 30 minutes per day — more than any other intervention tested.

The practical version of this is simple:

On the Sunday after the change, get outside within an hour of waking. Even on a cloudy day, outdoor light is 10 to 100 times brighter than indoor lighting. A 10-minute walk will do more to reset your internal clock than any amount of incremental bedtimes.

The rest of the preparation is about protecting the margins, not engineering a perfect transition.

An honest rule of thumb

Start the week before the change by waking at your usual time — don't sleep in on the weekend. That's it. That's the single most important thing you can do.

If you want to do more, here is what the evidence actually supports:

  • Push your bedtime by 15 minutes for three nights before the shift — only if you can also advance your wake time by the same amount. Doing one without the other is pointless.
  • Eat a normal breakfast at the same time each morning. Meal timing is a secondary circadian signal. Consistent morning eating reinforces the wake signal.
  • Dim lights 90 minutes before bed in the three nights prior. This is not about "sleep hygiene" in the vague sense. It is about preventing light-driven delays that would counteract the advance you need.
  • If you use caffeine, keep the morning dose consistent. Don't shift it earlier to compensate for fatigue. You want as much adenosine pressure as possible at your new bedtime.

What not to bother with: napping strategically, blackout curtains (you want morning light, not less of it), or "sleep banking." The common advice to stockpile sleep beforehand reflects a misunderstanding of how sleep debt works. Sleep is not a savings account.

What about fall-back?

The November transition is genuinely easier for most people. The research reflects this — no post-transition heart attack spike, fewer ER visits, fewer traffic accidents. Your body naturally wants to delay, so gaining an hour is more accommodating.

But there is a catch: the extra hour of sleep often doesn't happen. People stay up later because they feel they have an extra hour, then wake up at their usual time anyway. The net effect is a slightly delayed circadian phase that takes a day or two to normalize.

The intervention for fall-back is the reverse of spring. Morning light is less urgent. Evening light becomes the risk. If you stay in bright indoor light until midnight, you delay your clock further. If you want to reset quickly after fall-back, dim the lights after dinner for two nights and get morning light at your usual time.

The part the advice usually misses

Most DST preparation content assumes the reader has perfect control over their schedule. The truth is that parents of young children, shift workers, and people with early-morning commutes cannot choose when they wake up. If your alarm is fixed, the 15-minute incremental approach is not just ineffective — it's impossible to execute.

For these readers, the honest answer is less satisfying: accept the disruption, prioritize morning light on the days you can, and know that your clock will re-entrain within five to seven days regardless of what you do. The fear around DST — heart attacks, accidents, mood disruption — is real but concentrated among people with pre-existing vulnerability. For most healthy adults, the spring transition costs you a few days of grogginess and maybe one or two noticeably bad nights.

That is not trivial. But it is also not a catastrophe, and it does not require elaborate preparation.

The single best thing you can do for daylight saving time preparation is to stop treating it as a sleep emergency and start treating it as a light-exposure problem. Walk outside on Sunday morning. Dim the lights on Sunday night. Eat breakfast at the same time. Do that for three days, and your body will catch up.

The advice you've been hearing — go to bed 15 minutes earlier for a week — isn't wrong. It's just aimed at the wrong variable. The clock you need to adjust is not on your nightstand. It's in the sky.
