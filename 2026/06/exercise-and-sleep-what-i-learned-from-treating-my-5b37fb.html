Exercise and Sleep: What I Learned From Treating My Workout Like a Sleep-Prescription

June 18, 2026 5 min read By Samuel Kenworth

For two weeks, I treated my evening workout the way I treat a sleep medication: same time, same dose, same logging of results.

I had read the consensus — that exercise and sleep share a bidirectional relationship, each influencing the other — but I wanted to know if the type of exercise mattered as much as the timing, and whether the effect was large enough to feel, not just to measure.

So I logged 14 nights. Same bedtime (10:45 PM). Same wake time (6:15 AM). Same sleep environment (67 °F, blackout curtains, no phone in the room). The only variable was the workout: its type, its intensity, and how far it ended from lights-out.

Here is what the science says, what I found, and where the two agree.

What the Research Actually Shows

The relationship between exercise and sleep is not a simple "move more, sleep better" line. It is specific, dose-dependent, and mediated by timing.

Aerobic exercise — continuous movement that elevates heart rate and respiration — has the strongest evidence base. A 2015 meta-analysis in the Journal of Behavioral Medicine pooled 13 randomized controlled trials and found that regular moderate aerobic exercise reduced sleep-onset latency by an average of 11 minutes and improved subjective sleep quality on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index by roughly 2 points. The mechanism is not fully settled, but the leading hypothesis involves exercise-induced increases in core body temperature followed by a steeper post-exercise temperature drop, which signals the brain to initiate sleep.

Resistance training has been studied less extensively, but the available data is promising. A 2020 randomized trial in Sleep found that 12 weeks of progressive resistance training (three sessions per week) produced improvements in sleep efficiency comparable to aerobic training in adults with insomnia. The effect appeared to be independent of cardiovascular conditioning — suggesting that muscular fatigue itself, or the metabolic signaling it triggers, plays a role.

Yoga and low-intensity movement belong in a separate category. Several small trials have shown that regular yoga practice reduces subjective insomnia severity, but the effect may be driven more by the parasympathetic nervous system activation (slowing heart rate, lowering cortisol) than by physical exertion. A 2012 systematic review in Clinical Psychology Review noted that while yoga interventions consistently improved self-reported sleep, the studies rarely controlled for the breathing and mindfulness components, making it difficult to isolate movement from relaxation.

Timing matters, but not how you think. The old advice — don't exercise within a few hours of bedtime — has been softened. A 2019 meta-analysis in Sports Medicine of 23 studies found no significant sleep disruption from evening exercise performed 60 to 90 minutes before bed, provided the exercise was not extremely vigorous (near-maximal effort) and the individual was not prone to exercise-induced anxiety. High-intensity interval training within one hour of bedtime did, however, increase heart rate variability latency in some participants.

What I Found in 14 Nights

I tested three workout categories: moderate aerobic (30–40 minutes of cycling at 130–145 bpm), heavy resistance (45 minutes of compound lifts at 75–85 % of one-rep max), and evening yoga (30 minutes of slow vinyasa, ending at least 90 minutes before bed). Each category I did four times across the two weeks. Two nights were rest controls.

Sleep-onset latency.

On aerobic nights, I fell asleep in an average of 9 minutes. On resistance nights, 13 minutes. On yoga nights, 11 minutes. On rest nights, 22 minutes.

The caveat: my rest nights followed sedentary days with no physical activity. That 22-minute average might partly reflect a body that hadn't accumulated enough movement pressure to feel physiologically ready for sleep.

Wake-after-sleep-onset (WASO).

This metric is harder to capture without polysomnography, but using a simple log of estimated awake time during the night, I recorded an average of 8 minutes of mid-night wakefulness after aerobic days, 12 after resistance days, 5 after yoga days, and 17 after rest days.

The yoga number caught my attention. Those nights I drifted off relatively quickly (11 minutes) and rarely woke before morning. It is possible that the slow breathing and body scanning that accompanied the yoga — not the movement itself — produced that effect.

Subjective refreshment.

I rated each morning on a 1–5 scale (1 = groggy, 5 = fully rested). Average: aerobic 3.8, resistance 3.5, yoga 4.2, rest 2.6. The yoga score was unexpectedly high. I suspect it reflects the combination of mild physical activity plus deliberate nervous-system downregulation, rather than the exercise alone.

The timing variable that mattered most.

I tracked the interval between workout end and lights-out across all 14 nights. The correlation was not linear; instead, there appeared to be a sweet spot. Workouts ending 2 to 4 hours before bedtime produced the best sleep outcomes across all metrics. Workouts ending within 90 minutes of bed were slightly worse for sleep-onset latency, even when the exercise itself was low-intensity. And workouts ending more than 6 hours before bed seemed to lose their sleep benefit entirely, at least for onset latency.

That last finding matches the thermoregulatory hypothesis: if the post-exercise core temperature drop has already normalized by bedtime, the signal to initiate sleep is weaker.

Who This Is For — and Who It Isn't

If you already exercise but haven't connected it to your sleep, the data here suggests you can optimize the link by doing three things: keep the workout moderate rather than punishing, end it 2–4 hours before bed, and on nights when you need reliable sleep, consider low-intensity movement over high-exertion training.

If you do not exercise at all and have mild sleep issues, moderate aerobic activity appears to be the best entry point. The evidence for it is strongest, the subjective improvement is noticeable, and the risk of disrupting sleep with poor timing is lower than with heavy resistance work.

If you have diagnosed insomnia, exercise is not a replacement for cognitive behavioral therapy, but it may be an effective adjunct. The 2020 resistance-training trial mentioned above showed improvements in sleep efficiency even in clinical insomnia patients — but the effect size was modest, and individual responses varied widely.

This is a sample size of one, and I am not a controlled experiment. My stress levels, diet, and caffeine timing were not held constant. But the pattern across 14 nights was consistent enough to change my behavior: I now schedule my hardest workouts for early evening and end them at least two hours before bed, and I keep a short yoga sequence on nights when I need to sleep well the next morning.

The Myth and the Reality

The old line — exercise keeps you up at night because it is stimulating — is not wrong, but it is incomplete.

The more accurate version: exercise can improve sleep onset, depth, and continuity, provided it is dosed appropriately, timed deliberately, and matched to the individual. When it is, the effect is as reliable as any non-pharmaceutical intervention I have tried.
