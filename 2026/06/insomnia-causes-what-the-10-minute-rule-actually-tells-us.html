Insomnia Causes: What the 10-Minute Rule Actually Tells Us

June 01, 2026 12 min read By Emma Stanfield

If you have looked up insomnia causes in the last twenty years, you have almost certainly encountered a number: 10 minutes. The rule is simple — if you are in bed for more than 10 minutes without falling asleep, get up. Do something boring. Come back when you are tired again. It is the single most widely circulated behavioral recommendation for insomnia in the English-speaking world, repeated by sleep specialists, primary care doctors, and the National Health Service.

The 10-minute rule has a specific origin. It comes from a treatment called stimulus control therapy, developed in the 1970s by the psychologist Richard Bootzin. Bootzin's logic was elegant. The bed should be a cue for sleep, not for wakefulness. If you spend hours in bed awake, your brain starts to associate the bed with frustration, rumination, alertness — the opposite of what you need. So you break the association by limiting time in bed to actual sleep, and by getting up when sleep does not come within a set window.

Bootzin's original protocol used 10 minutes. That is where the number comes from. It was not derived from a study that measured exactly how long people should wait. It was a clinical heuristic — a reasonable, testable starting point.

What the research actually measured is a little different from what the rule implies. Bootzin and colleagues tested stimulus control in a 1972 study with 12 participants. The results were promising: those who followed the protocol fell asleep faster and woke up less during the night. But the study did not compare 10 minutes to 5 minutes or 20 minutes. It compared stimulus control to no treatment. The 10 minutes worked as part of a package, not because 10 is a magic number.

Later research has been more careful. A 2017 systematic review by Koffel et al. in the journal Sleep Medicine Reviews examined 14 trials of stimulus control and found it consistently reduced sleep-onset latency — the time it takes to fall asleep — by an average of 10 to 20 minutes across studies. But none of those trials tested whether 10 minutes was the optimal threshold. The 10-minute rule persists because it is simple to teach, easy to remember, and it solves a real problem: the spiral of lying in bed getting more anxious about not sleeping while not sleeping.

The number is useful. But the number is also a distraction if it makes you think insomnia is simply a matter of bad bedtime habits that can be fixed with a timer.

What the 10-minute rule does not measure

The rule does not tell you why you are lying there awake in the first place. It is a behavioral intervention for a condition that is rarely purely behavioral. Insomnia is a symptom with many roots, and stimulus control only addresses one piece of it: the learned association between bed and wakefulness. If the underlying driver is something else, the 10-minute rule is like resetting a smoke alarm while the wiring is still frayed.

Here is what the research points to as the actual drivers.

The hyperarousal model: why your brain will not dim the lights

The most consistent physiological finding in people with chronic insomnia is that they are not relaxed. They look tired. They feel tired. But measured objectively, their nervous systems are running at a higher baseline than controls. This is the hyperarousal model of insomnia, first formalized by Bonnet and Arand in a 1997 paper in the Journal of Sleep Research, and it has held up well in the decades since.

Hyperarousal means the sympathetic nervous system — the branch responsible for fight-or-flight — is more active, even during the day. People with insomnia have higher resting heart rates, higher cortisol levels in the evening than people who sleep normally, and faster metabolic rates while asleep. Electroencephalography (EEG) studies show that the brains of people with insomnia do not produce the same slow-wave activity during sleep. They are not sleeping deeply enough, and part of the reason is that the central nervous system is not disengaging the way it should.

The cause of this hyperarousal is not always stress in the obvious sense. In some individuals, it appears to be trait-like — a constitutional tendency toward light, fragmented sleep that becomes self-reinforcing. In others, it is directly triggered by a period of acute stress that persists beyond the stressor. The common citation here is the Spielman model, sometimes called the 3P model: predisposing factors (your baseline vulnerability to insomnia), precipitating factors (the stressful event that tips you over), and perpetuating factors (the behaviors and thoughts that keep the problem going once the initial stress is gone).

The hyperarousal model explains why sleep hygiene alone rarely cures chronic insomnia. You cannot will your sympathetic nervous system to quiet down. Telling someone with a racing resting heart rate to "try relaxing" is not helpful. They would relax if they could. The system has lost its off switch.

The circadian piece: timing, light, and the phase problem

A second major category of insomnia causes is circadian. The body's internal clock — the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a tiny cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus — sets a daily rhythm for alertness and sleep. That rhythm is not infinitely flexible. It has a natural period of about 24 hours and 10 minutes in most adults, and it is locked to the external day primarily through light exposure to the eyes.

When the timing of your sleep schedule does not match the timing of your internal clock, you get what is called circadian misalignment. You can be exhausted and still unable to fall asleep because the clock is signaling alertness, not sleepiness. This is the biological explanation for the person who falls asleep at 2 a.m. without difficulty but cannot fall asleep at 11 p.m. no matter how tired they are.

A 2019 study by Skarke et al. in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences used a strictly controlled inpatient protocol to map the circadian rhythms of a small sample of healthy adults. They found that sleep efficiency — the percentage of time in bed actually spent asleep — dropped significantly when sleep was attempted at the wrong circadian phase. That sounds obvious, but the implication is specific: for some people, what looks like insomnia is actually a circadian phase delay. The clock is working fine. It is just set to a later time zone.

This is particularly relevant for adolescents and young adults, whose circadian periods naturally lengthen during puberty, and for older adults, whose circadian amplitude — the strength of the daily signal — diminishes with age. It is also relevant for anyone who lives under artificial light that does not dim in the evening. Light suppresses melatonin release from the pineal gland. Blue-wavelength light is the most suppressive, but any light above about 30 lux at eye level will have some effect. A typical living room is around 100 lux. A phone screen held at reading distance is about 80 to 100 lux.

The practical difference between circadian-driven insomnia and hyperarousal-driven insomnia matters because the recommendations diverge. Circadian problems respond better to timed light exposure and consistent wake times than to wind-down routines. Hyperarousal responds better to interventions that directly address the sympathetic activation — cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), for example, which includes stimulus control but also cognitive restructuring and relaxation training.

The medical and medication drivers that get missed

A third category is the one most likely to be invisible to the person experiencing the insomnia. Several medical conditions directly disrupt sleep architecture, and several medications do the same, often in ways that are not listed on the bottle.

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) and periodic limb movement disorder (PLMD) are the most commonly overlooked causes of sleep-maintenance insomnia. RLS involves an uncomfortable urge to move the legs during rest, typically worse in the evening. PLMD involves repetitive leg movements during sleep that the sleeper does not consciously experience but that repeatedly pull them out of deeper sleep stages. A 2015 meta-analysis by Hening et al. in Sleep Medicine estimated that RLS affects about 7 to 10 percent of the North American population, with prevalence increasing with age and in women. The same analysis found that people with moderate to severe RLS lost an average of 60 to 90 minutes of sleep per night compared to controls.

The link to insomnia is straightforward: if your legs are moving involuntarily every 20 to 40 seconds during the night, you do not get consolidated sleep. You wake up feeling unrefreshed, and you may not know why. RLS and PLMD are treatable — often with iron supplementation if ferritin levels are low (a common scenario), or with medications that reduce the limb movements — but they are underdiagnosed because neither patient nor doctor thinks to ask about leg sensations or kicking during the night.

Obstructive sleep apnea is another major source of insomnia that does not always present with the classic symptom of loud snoring. The apnea-hypopnea index — the number of breathing pauses per hour of sleep — can be high enough to fragment sleep without the person ever noticing they stopped breathing. A 2013 study by Lichstein et al. in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine found that among older adults with insomnia in a community sample, about 30 percent also had clinically significant sleep apnea. The two conditions coexist more often than is commonly recognized.

Medications that can cause or worsen insomnia include:

  • Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) — particularly fluoxetine and sertraline, which can be activating and delay sleep onset
  • Beta-blockers — which reduce melatonin production and can cause nightmares or frequent awakenings
  • Corticosteroids — which suppress slow-wave sleep and increase nighttime cortisol
  • Decongestants containing pseudoephedrine — a stimulant that can persist for hours
  • Thyroid hormone replacement — if the dose is too high, it mimics hyperthyroidism and prevents sleep consolidation

The list is not exhaustive, but the pattern is worth noting. If insomnia started around the same time a medication was started or increased, the medication is a plausible cause. That does not mean it should be stopped — stopping some of these medications abruptly is dangerous. But it means the insomnia should be evaluated in context, not treated as a separate problem.

Hormonal insomnia in pregnancy and perimenopause

The hormonal drivers of insomnia are poorly suited to the 10-minute rule because the problem is not behavioral. It is chemical.

During pregnancy, progesterone levels rise dramatically. Progesterone has a sedating effect in the first trimester — many women report sleeping more — but by the second and third trimesters, other factors dominate. The enlarging uterus causes physical discomfort and frequent urination. Nighttime reflux becomes more common as progesterone relaxes the esophageal sphincter. Restless legs syndrome emerges or worsens in about one-third of pregnant women, especially in the third trimester, partly due to iron and folate demands of the fetus.

But the hormonal effect on sleep architecture itself is real. A 2018 systematic review by Mindell et al. in Obstetrics & Gynecology examined sleep across pregnancy in over 500 women in a controlled laboratory setting. The study found that total sleep time decreased progressively from the first to the third trimester, while nighttime awakenings increased threefold. Sleep efficiency — the percentage of time in bed actually asleep — dropped from about 85 percent in early pregnancy to below 75 percent by the third trimester. For context, a sleep efficiency below 85 percent is considered poor.

The hormonal contribution is not limited to pregnancy. Perimenopause — the transitional years before menopause, typically ages 45 to 55 — is associated with a sharp increase in insomnia prevalence. The common citation here is the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN), a large multisite study that followed over 3,000 women through the menopause transition. SWAN data published in Sleep in 2007 by Kravitz et al. found that perimenopausal and postmenopausal women were significantly more likely to report trouble staying asleep than premenopausal women, even after adjusting for age, depression, and hot flashes.

The mechanism appears to involve the decline in estradiol. Estradiol promotes the production of the neurotransmitter serotonin, which is a precursor to melatonin. It also modulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis — the body's central stress response system. When estradiol drops, the HPA axis becomes more reactive to stress, which in practical terms means a lower threshold for nighttime cortisol release and nighttime awakening.

The 10-minute rule is not useless for these women. Getting out of bed during a nighttime awakening and doing something quiet for a few minutes is still sound advice. But it treats the symptom, not the driver.

Sleep in older adults: not less need, less ability

There is a persistent cultural assumption that older adults need less sleep. The data do not support this. The National Sleep Foundation's 2015 consensus statement — prepared by Hirshkowitz et al. in Sleep Health — recommended 7 to 8 hours of sleep for adults over 65, the same range as for adults aged 26 to 64. But older adults are less able to get that sleep, and the reasons are physiological, not behavioral.

The aging brain produces less slow-wave sleep — the deep, restorative stage of sleep that is most closely associated with subjective refreshment. Slow-wave sleep declines by about 2 to 3 percent per decade starting in the 30s, and by age 70, many adults spend very little time in this stage. The circadian system also weakens. The suprachiasmatic nucleus loses neurons with age, and the amplitude of the circadian signal diminishes. This leads to earlier bedtimes, earlier wake times, and more fragmented sleep — a pattern known as advanced sleep phase syndrome.

Additionally, the bladder's capacity decreases with age, and the hormone vasopressin, which typically reduces urine production at night, is less effective. Nocturia — waking to urinate — becomes the single most common cause of nighttime awakening in older adults. A 2016 study by Bliwise et al. in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society followed 279 adults over 60 and found that nocturia accounted for more than half of all nighttime awakenings. Treating the nocturia — by restricting evening fluids, treating an enlarged prostate, or adjusting diuretic timing — often improves sleep more than any direct sleep intervention.

The clinical implication is that insomnia in older adults should not be assumed to be "normal aging" and left untreated. But it also should not be treated with the same behavioral approach used for a 30-year-old with stress-related insomnia. The driver is different, and the intervention should match the driver.

What the research says about the 10-minute rule today

Returning to the number: the 10-minute rule is still recommended by most clinical guidelines for insomnia, including the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 practice parameters. The evidence behind it is strongest as part of a multicomponent treatment — CBT-I — rather than as a standalone rule. Used alone, it helps some people and does not help others. The difference is usually about whether the underlying problem is the kind of conditioned arousal that stimulus control was designed to fix.

A 2014 study by Espie et al. in the Lancet — one of the largest randomized trials of CBT-I ever conducted, with over 1,600 participants — found that the full CBT-I package produced significant improvements in insomnia severity, sleep efficiency, and sleep-onset latency compared to usual care. Stimulus control was one component. But the study also found that the people who improved most were those who had a specific profile: high cognitive arousal (racing thoughts at bedtime) and low circadian disruption. For people whose insomnia was driven by pain, medical illness, or circadian misalignment, the effect was smaller.

This is what the 10-minute rule does not tell you, and why a single number can only take you so far. The rule is a tool. Whether it works depends on what is causing the insomnia.

An honest rule of thumb

If you are lying in bed awake, do not lie there for more than 20 to 25 minutes before getting up. The 10-minute threshold is too tight for most people to actually follow — it creates its own anxiety. Give yourself a reasonable window, and if sleep does not come, get up and do something quiet in low light for 10 to 15 minutes. Return to bed only when you feel actual drowsiness — not just tiredness, but the specific heaviness of eyelids that signals an approaching sleep onset. This preserves the bed-sleep association without the stopwatch pressure.

If this does not improve your sleep over two weeks, the problem is unlikely to be about bed-sleep association. It is time to look upstream.

What I have stopped doing

I see people who have been using the 10-minute rule for years, and they are often worse than when they started. They have internalized the idea that if they cannot fall asleep in 10 minutes, they have failed some test. The rule has become another source of performance anxiety.

I have stopped recommending a specific number to anyone who asks. Instead, I ask them what happens in the 20 minutes before they get into bed. That is usually where the real story is — what they were reading, how bright the light was, whether they were arguing with someone in their head before their head hit the pillow. The number at the bedside is a detail. The number at the bedside is never the whole picture.
