Is Sleep Apnea Surgery Better Than CPAP? What Surgery Can and Can’t Fix

June 01, 2026 5 min read By Sophie Langford

The question arrives in the middle of the third week, usually around 2 a.m., when the mask is on the floor and the machine is humming uselessly at a ghost: Can't they just operate on this? Why am I strapping a turbine to my face every night when people get their tonsils out and breathe fine?

It's a fair question. Sleep apnea surgery exists. Some of it works remarkably well — for the right person. For everyone else, the phrase "surgery" implies a permanence and a certainty that the evidence simply does not support. Understanding the gap between what surgery promises and what it actually delivers is the difference between a second opinion and a second procedure.

What Sleep Apnea Surgery Actually Addresses

Obstructive sleep apnea is a mechanical collapse of the pharyngeal airway during sleep. The collapse can happen at multiple points — the soft palate, the base of the tongue, the lateral pharyngeal walls, the epiglottis. Most people with moderate-to-severe OSA have collapse at more than one level. This is the central problem for surgery: you can stiffen, trim, or reposition one segment, but the others can still give way.

A 2019 meta-analysis in JAMA Otolaryngology reviewing surgical outcomes across 44 studies found that the average AHI reduction after pharyngeal surgery was roughly 50 percent, but individual results varied enormously by patient anatomy and disease severity. The same paper noted that "surgical success" is often defined as an AHI below 20 with at least a 50 percent reduction from baseline — a bar considerably lower than the AHI under 5 that CPAP routinely achieves.

The Main Surgical Options, Ranked by Evidence

Uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP)

The oldest and most common procedure. The surgeon removes excess tissue from the soft palate, uvula, and sometimes the tonsils. Mechanism: stiffen the palate so it doesn't flutter into the airway on inhalation.

Best candidate: Someone with isolated retropalatal collapse, mild-to-moderate OSA, and a BMI under 30. A drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) should confirm that the collapse is happening at the palate and nowhere else.

Evidence ceiling: Long-term success rates (AHI < 20 plus 50 percent reduction) hover around 40 to 50 percent at one year, with some regression over time. The reoperation rate for residual or recurrent OSA is not trivial.

Caveat: UPPP does not address tongue-base collapse. If your apnea is driven by the tongue falling back, a palatal procedure alone may reduce snoring without meaningfully improving oxygen desaturation events — which can create a false sense of security.

Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation (Inspire)

A surgically implanted device that stimulates the hypoglossal nerve during sleep, causing the tongue to protrude and keep the airway patent. The patient uses a handheld remote to turn it on at bedtime. It is the closest thing surgery has to a "CPAP replacement" because it actively opens the airway rather than passively removing tissue.

Best candidate: Moderate-to-severe OSA (AHI 15–65), BMI under 32 (some centers now accept up to 35), and confirmed tongue-base collapse without complete concentric palatal collapse on DISE. These are narrow criteria. A 2022 review in Otolaryngologic Clinics of North America estimated that only about 30 percent of OSA patients meet the anatomical and BMI requirements.

Evidence ceiling: The STAR trial, which followed patients for five years, reported a median AHI reduction from 32 to 9, with therapy adherence (measured as hours of use per night) exceeding 80 percent at five years. That adherence rate is dramatically higher than CPAP adherence, which is why some clinicians now consider it first-line for eligible patients.

Caveat: It's expensive ($30,000–$40,000 in the U.S.), requires two surgeries (implant and removal when the battery depletes), and is not an option for people with central sleep apnea or certain implantable-device contraindications.

Maxillomandibular Advancement (MMA)

The most invasive option and also the most effective. The surgeon cuts the upper and lower jaws, moves them forward, and fixes them in the new position. This expands the entire pharyngeal airway, not just one segment.

Best candidate: Severe OSA with craniofacial abnormalities (retrognathia, midface deficiency) in otherwise healthy surgical candidates. Also used as a salvage procedure when other surgeries have failed.

Evidence ceiling: MMA routinely achieves AHI reductions of 80–90 percent, and many patients are effectively cured (AHI under 5). A 2023 systematic review in the Journal of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery reported a surgical success rate of 86 percent across 28 studies.

Caveat: Recovery is brutal — liquid diet for weeks, facial swelling for months, permanent numbness in the lower lip and chin in most patients. This is not an alternative to CPAP for mild apnea.

Nasal Surgery, Tonsillectomy, and Other Targeted Procedures

These work best when a specific anatomical obstruction is identified. Isolated nasal valve collapse causing snoring but not significant apnea? Nasal surgery may help. Massive tonsils in a thin young adult? Tonsillectomy alone can resolve the apnea. These are legitimate but narrow indications.

When Surgery Beats CPAP (and When It Doesn't)

Surgery wins on one measure unequivocally — adherence. CPAP adherence at one year hovers around 30 to 50 percent depending on the definition used. Hypoglossal nerve stimulator adherence at five years exceeds 80 percent. If the machine is gathering dust on your nightstand, a properly selected surgical option will provide more therapy than your unused CPAP ever could.

Surgery loses on predictability. With CPAP, you know the pressure, you know the AHI result, and you can titrate it in a single night. With surgery, you are asking a surgeon to render a dynamic three-dimensional airway permanently patent using a procedure that works differently in every patient. The outcome is probabilistic, not guaranteed.

This is the unspoken deal: CPAP is a reliable inconvenience. Surgery is an unreliable cure.

Who This Is For

  • People with moderate-to-severe OSA who have tried CPAP for at least three months with documented poor adherence (fewer than four hours per night on more than 70 percent of nights).
  • People with a confirmed single-level collapse on DISE.
  • People with severe OSA and craniofacial abnormalities who are willing to undergo MMA recovery.

Who This Isn't For

  • People with mild OSA who have not tried CPAP or an oral appliance. Surgery is not the first step.
  • People who are hoping surgery will "fix" apnea caused primarily by excess weight without addressing the underlying metabolic component. Weight loss of 10–15 percent of body weight can reduce AHI by 50 percent or more in some patients — and doesn't require an OR.
  • People with central or mixed sleep apnea. Surgery addresses the mechanical airway, not the brain's respiratory drive.

The Myth and the Truth

The myth: There is a surgery that can cure your sleep apnea so you never have to think about it again.

The truth: For a small, carefully selected subset of patients — those with single-level collapse, favorable anatomy, and disease severity that matches the procedure — surgery can eliminate the need for nightly therapy. For everyone else, surgery is a reduction strategy, not a resolution, and CPAP or an oral appliance remains the more reliable tool.

The better question is not "Can surgery replace CPAP?" but "What is the least burdensome intervention that will keep my airway open through the night?" That answer is different for every patient, and anyone who promises a single answer for everyone is selling something. The honest clinicians — and the honest evidence — admit where the certainty ends.
