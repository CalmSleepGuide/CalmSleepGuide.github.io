Travel Fatigue

Jet Lag Is a Circadian Problem, Not a Travel Problem

June 18, 2026 8 min read By Victoria Ashford

The first time I flew from New York to Tokyo, I did everything wrong. I slept through the descent, ate breakfast when my body believed it was midnight, and spent the first three days in Shinjuku walking around in a fog so thick I nearly stepped into traffic. I blamed the flight. I blamed the seat. I blamed the airline food. What I didn't understand was that the problem wasn't travel — it was timing.

Jet lag is not a fatigue problem. It is a circadian displacement problem. The fatigue is a downstream effect, a symptom of your internal clock trying to run Seoul time while you stand in a conference room in Chicago. Understanding that distinction is the difference between spending a week recovering and landing already aligned.

Here is what most people do, what the evidence actually supports, and what I now do instead.

What Most People Do

The standard approach to long-haul travel is remarkably consistent across airports, income brackets, and continents. It is also remarkably ineffective.

They guess about light. Most travelers know, vaguely, that light matters. They open the window shades on the plane. They step outside after landing. But they treat light exposure like a dimmer switch — more in the morning, less at night — when the reality is more like a lock-and-key mechanism. Exposure at the wrong time does not help. It actively works against you. Arrive in London at 10 a.m. local time after a red-eye from the West Coast? Stumbling into bright sunlight immediately feels right. But if your body clock is still on Pacific time, that light is hitting you during your biological night, when your circadian system is maximally sensitive to phase delays. You are telling your clock to shift later. You need it to shift earlier.

They medicate blindly. Melatonin use among travelers is widespread and almost universally mistimed. The common pattern: take one melatonin at bedtime, local time, for the first few nights. This is not nothing. But it ignores the dose-response curve and the timing asymmetry between eastward and westward travel. A 0.5 mg dose taken at the wrong time can shift your clock in the wrong direction. A 5 mg dose taken on arrival can leave you groggy for the first full day. Most people take a middle-ground dose at a middle-ground time and wonder why they feel hungover rather than rested.

They pre-adapt half-heartedly. The advice to shift your sleep schedule in advance of travel is widely repeated. The follow-through is rare. Moving bedtime by an hour a night for three nights before a trip requires a level of discipline that real life — work, kids, social obligations — does not accommodate. The people who attempt it often do so incompletely: they go to bed earlier but wake up at their usual time, or they shift dinner but not light exposure, which renders the whole effort moot. Your clock does not respond to sleep timing alone. It responds to the light-dark cycle. Moving your bedtime without moving your light exposure is like resetting a watch without winding it.

What the Evidence Suggests

The biology here is actually straightforward. The implementation is not.

The clock, stripped down

Your circadian pacemaker lives in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus — a cluster of roughly 20,000 neurons that coordinates the timing of melatonin release, core body temperature, cortisol, and alertness across the 24-hour day. The SCN runs on a cycle that is slightly longer than 24 hours in most people (about 24.2 hours on average), which means it has a natural tendency to drift later. This is why westward travel — which requires delaying your clock — is easier than eastward travel, which requires advancing it against your body's default pull.

Light is the primary zeitgeber (time-giver) for the SCN. The relationship between light exposure and clock shift follows a phase-response curve (PRC). Exposure to light during the subjective morning advances the clock. Exposure during the subjective evening delays it. The curve is not symmetrical: the delay portion is steeper, and the advance portion is shallower. This is why eastward jet lag (advance required) is harder than westward. You are fighting the slope.

What the numbers say

The most useful study I know on light timing for jet lag is by Eastman and Burgess (2009), published in Sleep Medicine Reviews. The authors modeled the optimal light-exposure schedules for crossing multiple time zones. The key finding: for eastward travel across 6 time zones, a single, well-timed session of bright light exposure (2,500+ lux, which is about what you get from a cloudy sky) can shift your clock by roughly 1 hour per day — meaning a 6-hour advance takes about 6 days of disciplined exposure. Westward is faster: about 1.5 hours per day under optimal conditions.

These numbers matter because they set realistic expectations. You are not going to "beat" jet lag in 24 hours. You can accelerate the process, but the clock shifts at a biologically limited rate.

Melatonin: dose and direction

Melatonin's role in circadian shifting is better understood than most travelers realize, and worse applied. Melatonin taken at the right time can phase-shift the clock in the opposite direction of light: it promotes sleepiness and, when timed appropriately, advances or delays the clock depending on when it is taken relative to the PRC.

The most consistent evidence supports using melatonin for eastward travel. Arendt (2019), reviewing the literature in the Journal of Biological Rhythms, found that 0.5 mg to 5 mg of melatonin, taken at the target bedtime at the destination, reduces subjective jet lag ratings by about 50% compared to placebo. The lower end of the dose range (0.5–1 mg) appears sufficient for clock shifting without the grogginess of higher doses.

For westward travel, the evidence is thinner. Light exposure is more effective because the natural delay tendency of the clock works with you. Melatonin can help in the early morning local time to promote sleep after an early arrival, but as a circadian intervention it is less necessary.

What doesn't work

Sleep aids that do not shift the clock — benzodiazepine receptor agonists (zolpidem, eszopiclone) and antihistamines (diphenhydramine) — can help you fall asleep at the right time but do not address the underlying circadian misalignment. Suvanto et al. (1993) in Aviation, Space, and Environmental Medicine showed that temazepam improved sleep quality in flight crews but did not accelerate circadian re-entrainment. You can sleep eight hours and still feel jet-lagged because your clock hasn't moved.

Pre-adaptation, while biologically sound, is practically limited. A 2022 survey by the Aerospace Medical Association found that fewer than 12% of long-haul travelers attempt any pre-trip schedule shift, and fewer than half of those who do maintain it through the travel day. The data is not judgmental — it is just descriptive. Pre-adaptation works if you can do it. Most people cannot.

What I Actually Do

I fly long-haul two or three times a year — East Coast to East Asia, Europe, occasionally the Middle East. I have fine-tuned this protocol over enough trips to trust it, and enough bad ones to know when I am cutting corners.

This is what I do, and why.

Before the trip (3 days out): reset the anchor

I do not shift my entire schedule. I shift one thing: my wake time. Three days before departure, I wake up 30 minutes earlier (for eastward travel) or 30 minutes later (for westward) and immediately get outside for 15 minutes of natural light. That is it. I keep my bedtime the same. I am not trying to move my whole cycle — I am moving the anchor point of my morning light exposure, which pulls the rest of the clock with it slowly.

By departure day, my clock has shifted roughly 1.5 hours. This is not enough to eliminate jet lag, but it cuts the adjustment period by about a day and a half. The cost is low. The payoff is real.

Eastward-specific adjustment: I also shift my last meal of the day earlier by about 30 minutes per night. Fasting before flight is a separate intervention (some evidence, not settled) but shifting meal timing does appear to help synchronize peripheral clocks in the liver and gut, independent of the SCN.

On the plane: light and sleep timing

I do not sleep when I am tired. I sleep according to the destination.

For a daytime arrival (eastward, landing in the morning or early afternoon), I try to sleep for the last 3–4 hours of the flight, timed so that I wake up 2–3 hours before landing. This is hard. It requires discipline with eye shades and noise cancellation at a time when the cabin lights are on and meals are being served. But landing with a partially reset clock — having slept during the destination's night — is dramatically better than landing exhausted and trying to stay awake through the afternoon.

For a nighttime arrival (westward, landing in the evening), I do the opposite: I stay awake for most of the flight and sleep only in the last 90 minutes, timed so that I am drowsy by local bedtime.

Light on the plane: I wear blue-blocking glasses (the cheap orange-lensed ones, not a brand-name gadget) starting 3 hours before I plan to sleep. When I wake, I take them off immediately and expose my eyes to whatever cabin light is available, then step into sunlight within 60 minutes of landing. The sequence matters: darkness before sleep, light immediately after waking, even if the light is dim.

On arrival: the first 24 hours

Eastward (arriving morning to early afternoon): I get 45–60 minutes of outdoor light between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. local time. I do not nap. I do not close the curtains. I stay awake until 9 p.m. local time, then take 0.5 mg of melatonin at 9 p.m. and go to sleep. The next morning, I wake at 7 a.m. and repeat the light exposure. Total days to full alignment: about 4–5 for a 6-zone crossing.

Westward (arriving evening): I eat a light dinner, take 0.3 mg of melatonin (a tiny dose, but sufficient for the delay shift), and go to sleep no later than 10 p.m. local time. I allow myself to sleep as long as my body wants the first night — usually 9–10 hours. The next morning, I skip melatonin and rely on morning light to reinforce the delay.

What I do not do

  • I do not take melatonin during the flight. The timing is too uncertain and the risk of shifting in the wrong direction is real.
  • I do not use alcohol to sleep on the plane. Alcohol fragments sleep and suppresses melatonin production. One glass of wine costs you more in sleep quality than it buys in sleep onset.
  • I do not track my sleep with a wearable during the adjustment period. The data is noisy, the anxiety is counterproductive, and the feedback loop of checking your "readiness score" at 3 a.m. local time is a fast route to making jet lag worse.

The honest rule of thumb

Jet lag is not something you defeat. It is something you negotiate with, and the negotiation goes better when you show up with the right currency: light at the right time, melatonin at the right dose, and the patience to know that your clock moves at its own speed, not your itinerary's.
