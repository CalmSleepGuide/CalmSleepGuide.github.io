Pregnancy Insomnia

Pregnancy Sleep: What Actually Changes (and What You Can Do About It)

June 17, 2026 6 min read By Margaret Sullivan

You expect to lose sleep after the baby arrives. What no one warns you is that the insomnia often starts months earlier, while you are still the one being carried.

Pregnancy sleep disruption is not a failure of will or habit. It is the predictable result of three distinct problems that converge in the second and third trimesters: physical discomfort that makes every position feel wrong, breathing changes that fragment your sleep without your knowing it, and a mind that suddenly decides 3 AM is the perfect time to review your entire birth plan. Standard sleep advice — keep the room cool, put the phone away, go to bed at the same time — assumes a body that still belongs to you. A pregnant body needs different answers.

Let's look at each problem separately, compare the strategies that actually have evidence behind them, and see which ones are worth your limited energy.

The Position Problem: Three Sleep Postures, Three Criteria

The standard advice is to sleep on your left side. The reason is physiological: the inferior vena cava, the large vein returning blood to your heart, runs along the right side of your spine. By the third trimester, the weight of the uterus can compress this vein when you lie on your back, reducing cardiac output by 10 to 20 percent in some women (Jeffreys et al., 2006, Anaesthesia). The left-side-lying position minimizes this compression and optimizes blood flow to the placenta.

But "just sleep on your left side" ignores the fact that staying in one position all night is nearly impossible, and that side-sleeping can aggravate hip pain, shoulder strain, and acid reflux. So let's compare three options honestly.

Left-side sleeping wins on circulation — that's well-established. But it scores poorly on pelvic comfort for many women. The hip of the bottom leg bears your full weight, and the round ligament on that side can ache by morning. On acid reflux, side-sleeping is neutral to slightly helpful, since gravity keeps stomach contents down better than lying flat.

Back-sleeping is the position most pregnant women default to when they wake up. The data on this is thinner than the confidence with which it's usually condemned. The common citation is that supine sleep in late pregnancy is associated with lower birth weight and stillbirth risk in some observational studies (Stacey et al., 2011, BMJ). What is less often mentioned is that the absolute risk is very small, and that your body has protective mechanisms — namely, you will likely shift position or wake up before compression becomes dangerous. If you wake on your back, simply roll to your side. The evidence does not support panicking.

Semi-reclined sleeping — propped up on pillows at a 30- to 45-degree angle — is the underappreciated third option. It scores well on reflux (gravity helps), reasonably on circulation (the angle reduces uterine compression), and better than side-sleeping on pelvic comfort, because your hips bear less direct weight. The trade-off is that it can strain your lower back if the pillow support isn't right.

The verdict: Use left-side sleeping as your target position for falling asleep, but arrange your pillows so that semi-reclined is a comfortable backup. A pillow between the knees and a second one tucked behind your lower back can make side-sleeping tolerable. And if you revert to your back, just roll over. The goal is not perfection — it is minimizing the time you spend in any position that feels wrong.

The Breathing Problem: Two Strategies, One Clear Winner

Snoring that you never had before pregnancy is not just annoying. It is a signal that your airway is narrowing. Progesterone relaxes smooth muscle throughout the body, including the walls of your upper airway. Add nasal congestion from increased blood volume, and your airway can narrow enough to trigger sleep-disordered breathing — even in women with no history of it. About 35 percent of pregnant women develop new-onset snoring by the third trimester (Pien et al., 2014, Sleep Medicine Reviews), and a subset of those will have gestational sleep apnea.

Let's compare two strategies.

Positional therapy — sleeping on your side rather than your back — is the simplest intervention. Side-sleeping keeps the airway more open than supine sleep does, and it works for mild cases. The advantage is that it costs nothing and has no side effects. The disadvantage is that it only helps if your airway collapse is purely positional. For many women, it is not enough.

Elevated posture — sleeping with the head of the bed raised 30 degrees (or using a wedge pillow) — addresses the problem more directly. Elevation reduces the gravitational collapse of the soft palate and tongue base, and it also helps with nasal congestion by reducing venous pooling in the upper airway. A small but well-designed study found that 30-degree elevation improved oxygen saturation and reduced snoring in pregnant women with sleep-disordered breathing (Wilson et al., 2017, Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine). It is safe, easy, and more consistently effective than side-sleeping alone.

The verdict: Start with side-sleeping. If you still snore, wake up gasping, or feel unrested despite adequate time in bed, add head-of-bed elevation. This is not a luxury — it is a legitimate intervention for a physiological problem. If symptoms persist, a home sleep test or referral to a sleep medicine specialist is appropriate. Untreated sleep apnea in pregnancy is associated with gestational hypertension and preeclampsia (Louis et al., 2019, American Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology), so this is worth taking seriously.

The Racing-Mind Problem: Three Wind-Down Routines Compared

The 3 AM anxiety spiral is not a character flaw. It is what happens when your body is uncomfortable, your cortisol is naturally elevated in the third trimester, and your brain has uninterrupted time to review every unresolved question about labor, parenting, and life with a newborn. Standard sleep hygiene — no screens, no caffeine — does not address this.

Here is a comparison of three wind-down routines that have evidence behind them, judged on three criteria: how quickly they reduce sleep-onset latency (the time it takes to fall asleep), how practical they are to do alone in bed (without waking your partner), and what the dropout rate looks like in studies.

Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) — systematically tensing and releasing each muscle group from toes to face — has the strongest evidence for sleep-onset reduction. A meta-analysis of 12 trials found that PMR reduced sleep-onset latency by an average of 13 minutes in adults with insomnia (Williams et al., 2021, Sleep Medicine Reviews). It is easy to do in bed and requires no equipment. Dropout rates are low because the protocol is simple and takes about 10 minutes.

Scheduled worry time — setting aside 15 minutes earlier in the evening to write down every concern and then promising yourself you will not revisit them until the next scheduled session — has moderate evidence. A randomized trial of pregnant women with insomnia found that cognitive behavioral techniques including scheduled worry time improved sleep efficiency by about 8 percent over six weeks (Tomfohr-Madsen et al., 2017, Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology). The challenge is that it requires discipline: the worry brain does not respect a scheduled appointment.

Cognitive shuffling — a technique where you randomly name objects or words to occupy the brain's verbal processing centers and block rumination — has the thinnest evidence base. The common citation is a small pilot study (N=36) suggesting it reduced sleep-onset latency compared to no intervention, but replication is limited. It is easy to do in bed, but the evidence is not yet strong enough to recommend it over PMR.

The verdict: PMR is the most reliable first-line tool for racing thoughts at bedtime. It takes 10 minutes and is backed by meta-analytic evidence. Scheduled worry time is worth adding as a preventive measure earlier in the evening. Cognitive shuffling is plausible but thin — try it if PMR doesn't work for you, but don't expect it to be a consistent solution.

The Honest Rule of Thumb

If you only have energy for one change tonight: get your upper body elevated and your lower body supported. Prop yourself up on two pillows or a wedge so your head is above your chest, tuck a pillow between your knees, and do 10 minutes of progressive muscle relaxation before turning out the light. That combination addresses all three problems at once.

When to Call Someone

These strategies are for the common, manageable end of the pregnancy sleep spectrum. Call your provider if: you snore loudly enough that your partner notices pauses or gasping; you have chest discomfort or shortness of breath that does not resolve with position change; or you consistently lie awake for 30 minutes or more most nights despite trying the approaches above. Sleep apnea in pregnancy is treatable, and untreated insomnia can worsen postpartum mood disorders. You are not supposed to figure this out alone.

The baby will change your sleep soon enough. For now, the goal is not perfect nights — it is a few more good ones than you would have had otherwise.

You deserve those. And your body, as complicated as it is right now, can still learn to use them.
