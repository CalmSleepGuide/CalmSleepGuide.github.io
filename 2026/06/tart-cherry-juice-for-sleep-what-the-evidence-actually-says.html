Tart Cherry Juice for Sleep: What the Evidence Actually Says

June 03, 2026 5 min read By Patrick Sinclair

A friend tells you she drinks a glass of tart cherry juice before bed and now she sleeps through the night. You google it. You find a lot of articles using words like "natural melatonin bomb" and "cherry cure." You wonder if this is real or just another health-food story that sounds good and doesn't hold up.

Here is what the evidence actually says.

What Most People Do

The typical approach goes something like this: buy a bottle of tart cherry juice at the grocery store or Whole Foods. Drink one glass about thirty minutes before bed. Expect something to happen. If nothing noticeable happens after a few nights, move on to something else.

The dose is usually small — maybe four to six ounces. The timing is driven by convenience. And the expectation is that the juice works like a mild sedative: you drink it, you get sleepy.

None of this matches what the research actually tested.

The common dose in the published studies is about eight to twelve ounces of tart cherry juice — roughly one and a half standard servings — taken twice a day, in the morning and again in the late afternoon or early evening. Not once at bedtime. Not a small glass.

The idea that the juice works like a sleeping pill is also wrong. There is no evidence that tart cherry juice makes you drowsy within an hour. Its effects are subtler and work on a different timescale.

What the Evidence Suggests

The mechanism, in the order it happens

Tart cherries contain melatonin, but the amount is low. A 2012 analysis by Burkhardt and colleagues (Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry) measured melatonin concentrations in Montmorency cherries and found roughly 0.1 to 0.3 micrograms per gram of cherry. A typical eight-ounce glass of tart cherry juice might contain 0.5 to 2.0 micrograms of melatonin. For comparison, a standard over-the-counter melatonin supplement is 3000 micrograms — 1,500 times more.

If melatonin content were the mechanism, tart cherry juice would be essentially ineffective. So the explanation people usually give is incomplete.

The more plausible mechanism involves tryptophan. Tart cherries contain tryptophan, an amino acid that the body converts into serotonin and then into melatonin. But tryptophan alone is not the story either — dietary tryptophan competes with other amino acids for transport across the blood-brain barrier, which limits how much actually reaches the brain. What may matter is that tart cherry juice provides both tryptophan and carbohydrates. The carbohydrates trigger insulin release, which clears competing amino acids from the bloodstream, allowing more tryptophan to enter the brain. This is the same mechanism behind the old folk remedy of warm milk before bed — milk also contains tryptophan and lactose. The cherry version may be more effective because the tryptophan content is higher per serving.

Howland et al. (2011), writing in the Journal of Medicinal Food, tested this in a small pilot study with seven older adults who drank eight ounces of tart cherry juice twice daily for two weeks. They observed modest increases in time in bed, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency. The sample was too small to generalize, but it pointed toward a real effect.

Pigeon et al. (2010), in the same journal, ran a crossover trial with fifteen adults with insomnia. Participants drank eight ounces of tart cherry juice or a placebo juice twice daily for two weeks, then switched. The researchers measured sleep with actigraphy — a wrist-worn device that tracks movement. On the cherry juice, participants slept about 25 minutes longer per night and showed lower sleep fragmentation. That is a meaningful improvement for someone with mild-to-moderate insomnia.

A larger study by Losso et al. (2018), published in the American Journal of Therapeutics, followed eleven adults who drank a special tart cherry juice blend or a placebo for fourteen days. The cherry group showed a 36-minute increase in total sleep time. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov and used polysomnography — the gold-standard sleep measurement — rather than self-report.

The pattern across these studies is consistent: an increase in total sleep time of roughly 25 to 40 minutes, with improvements in sleep efficiency and fewer nighttime awakenings. The effect is not large — this is not a substitute for clinically significant insomnia treatment — but it is real.

What remains unknown

The research is so far limited to adults, mostly older adults or those with mild insomnia. No long-term studies exist. Nobody has tested whether the effects persist beyond two or three weeks, or whether tolerance develops. The studies used commercial tart cherry juice or proprietary blends, which makes it difficult to know what the minimum effective dose is or whether the results generalize across different products.

The common citation for dosing — eight to twelve ounces twice daily — comes from only a handful of small trials. It may be more than you need, or it may be exactly what you need. No one has run the dose-response study.

Timing matters more than people realize

The studies that showed an effect used a morning dose and a late-afternoon or early-evening dose — not a bedtime dose. The morning dose may help regulate the circadian system by providing melatonin precursors early in the day. The late-afternoon dose appears more directly involved in nighttime sleep. Drinking it too close to bedtime introduces a practical problem: eight to twelve ounces of liquid near sleep means you will likely wake up to urinate. That alone can undermine whatever sleep benefit the juice provides.

The better timing is probably around 4:00 to 6:00 PM for the second serving.

What I Actually Do

I keep a bottle of unsweetened tart cherry juice in the refrigerator. I pour about eight ounces into a small glass in the morning with breakfast. I pour another eight ounces around 5:00 PM, when I might otherwise reach for something caffeinated or mindlessly snack. I do not drink it within three hours of bed.

I do not expect it to produce a noticeable sleepy feeling. That is not the mechanism. I treat it as a small circadian signal, not a sedative. On nights when my sleep is already disrupted by stress or schedule, the juice does nothing. That is fine — it was never meant to fix those nights. On ordinary nights, I sleep about as well as I ever have, which for me means falling asleep within fifteen minutes and waking briefly once or twice before the alarm. Whether the juice contributes to that, I cannot say with certainty. But the cost is small, the evidence is plausible, and the habit of preparing it serves as a deliberate act that marks the transition out of the workday.

The one thing I would underline: if you try tart cherry juice, pay attention to sugar content. Many commercial brands add sugar. Unsweetened is the only version the evidence supports, because added sugar undermines the metabolic signal you are trying to produce. And if you take any medication — blood thinners, blood pressure drugs, MAO inhibitors — check with a doctor before adding significant amounts of cherry concentrate to your daily diet, because the interaction data is not complete.

The evidence for tart cherry juice is modest and the studies are small. But it is one of the few nutritional sleep aids that has any controlled data at all. That is worth something. The reason I keep drinking it is not that the studies convinced me. It is that I tried it, noticed nothing dramatic, realized I had been expecting the wrong thing, adjusted the timing and dose, and eventually stopped waiting for it to feel like anything. That is when it became useful.
