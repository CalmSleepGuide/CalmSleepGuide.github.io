The At-Home Sleep Study: What It Actually Measures (and What It Misses)

June 01, 2026 4 min read By Christopher Weston

You wake up with a dry mouth. Your partner tells you the snoring is getting worse — the kind where you stop breathing for a second and then gasp. Some mornings it takes an hour to feel human. The question floating in the room at breakfast: Is this sleep apnea?

The common answer now is an at-home sleep study. A small device arrives in the mail, you wear it to bed, send it back, and get a report. No hospital gown, no stranger watching you sleep, no night away from home. It's the obvious first move. But it helps to know exactly what you're getting — and what you're not.

Here are three ways to think about the decision.

What Most People Do

If you mention daytime fatigue and snoring to a primary care doctor in 2025, the most likely response is a home sleep apnea test (HSAT). The logic is hard to argue with: roughly 80% of obstructive sleep apnea cases are moderate to severe, and those are exactly the cases an at-home device catches reliably. The test is cheap — typically covered by insurance, $150–$300 if not — and it doesn't require a waitlist that stretches into months.

The hardware itself is straightforward. You get a small recording unit that sits on your chest, a nasal cannula that curls behind your ears (similar to the oxygen tubes in a hospital), a pulse oximeter that clips onto your finger, and sometimes a belt that straps around your abdomen to measure breathing effort. You put it on at bedtime, press a button to start recording, sleep as best you can, and return the device the next morning.

What the test measures: airflow, breathing effort, heart rate, and blood oxygen levels. From those four channels, the software calculates an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) — the average number of breathing pauses or partial obstructions per hour of recording time. An AHI of 5 to 15 is mild. Fifteen to 30 is moderate. Over 30 is severe.

The appeal is real. The device uses real hardware you can touch. The instructions are simple. And for a large number of people, the result is unambiguous — a high AHI, clear oxygen desaturations, and a prescription for a CPAP machine within two weeks.

What the Evidence Suggests

The research on home sleep tests is better than most people assume, but the confidence interval matters. A 2017 systematic review in the Annals of the American Thoracic Society (Kapur et al.) looked at HSAT accuracy across multiple studies and found that for patients with a high pre-test probability of moderate-to-severe OSA — people who snore loudly, have witnessed apneas, and score above 15 on the STOP-BANG questionnaire — the home test has a positive predictive value above 90%. In plain language: if the machine says you have significant sleep apnea, you almost certainly do.

The problem lives at the margins.

Home tests cannot measure sleep stages. They don't record brain waves (EEG), eye movements, or muscle tone. This matters more than it sounds like, because a standard in-lab polysomnogram counts apneas only during actual sleep. An at-home test counts them across the entire recording period — including time you spent awake, rolling around, trying to get comfortable with tubes on your face. The result is that mild cases get systematically undercounted. A person whose true AHI is 8 (mild OSA) might get a home-test result of 4 (technically normal), because the denominator includes wake time. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines explicitly recommend home testing only for uncomplicated adults with a high suspicion of moderate-to-severe OSA — not for screening the general population.

Central sleep apnea is also invisible to most home tests. The device can tell you stopped breathing. It cannot tell you whether the airway collapsed (obstructive) or your brain simply forgot to send the signal to breathe (central). The treatment for the two conditions is fundamentally different — CPAP for obstructive, adaptive servo-ventilation or positional therapy for central — so mistaking one for the other is not harmless.

A less discussed limitation: the hardware itself fails more often than lab equipment admits. The nasal cannula slips out of position in side sleepers. The pulse oximeter loses signal. The device records for five hours but the data is only interpretable for three. In clinic, a sleep technologist fixes these problems in real time. At home, you just lose data. Published failure rates for home tests range from 3% to 20% depending on the device and population studied — meaning as many as one in five patients may need a repeat test.

What I Actually Do

The honest protocol, if you or your partner are deciding tonight:

Step one: Do the STOP-BANG questionnaire yourself. It takes two minutes. You score one point each for Snoring, Tiredness during the day, Observed apnea, high blood Pressure, BMI over 35, Age over 50, Neck circumference over 40 cm (16 inches), and male Gender. A score of 5 or higher means high probability of moderate-to-severe OSA. Walk into your doctor's appointment with that number written down.

Step two: If your STOP-BANG is 5 or higher, the at-home test is the right first step. You will almost certainly get a clear result. The convenience wins. The cost wins. The time wins.

Step three: If your STOP-BANG is 3 or 4 — or if you have heart failure, atrial fibrillation, a history of stroke, or opioid use — push for the in-lab study. The mild-case undercounting problem and the central-apnea blind spot matter most in this territory. Tell your doctor: "I'm concerned my home test might miss mild OSA or central apnea. Can we go directly to polysomnography?" Some insurance plans require a failed home test first, but many will approve a lab study with a documented reason.

One more thing most guides skip: if you do the at-home test and your AHI comes back under 5 but your symptoms don't improve — if you're still exhausted, still waking with a headache, still being nudged by your partner — the test failed you, not the other way around. About 15% of people with symptomatic sleep apnea have a negative home test. Push for the lab. The honest rule of thumb: If your bed partner can reliably tell you that you stop breathing at night, trust that observation before you trust a device that counts breathing while you're awake — and if the test says you're fine but you still feel wrecked, that's not the end of the conversation, it's the beginning of a better one.
