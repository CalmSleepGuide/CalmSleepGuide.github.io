The At-Home Sleep Study: What It Actually Measures, What It Misses

June 18, 2026 5 min read By Thomas Whitfield

Eighty-eight percent is a good number on the surface. It sounds like a solid grade, a passing score, something you can rely on.

In sleep medicine, 88% is the pooled sensitivity of at-home sleep apnea testing for detecting moderate-to-obstructive sleep apnea — meaning if you have moderate or severe OSA, an at-home study will catch it roughly 88 times out of 100. That figure comes from a 2020 meta-analysis published in the journal Sleep, which combed through dozens of studies involving thousands of patients.

But that same analysis found something else: sensitivity for mild sleep apnea dropped to 70%. And specificity — the test's ability to correctly rule out the condition when it isn't there — was similarly uneven depending on who was being tested.

The number is useful. It is also incomplete. And understanding the difference is how you decide whether an at-home sleep study is right for you.

What the Home Test Actually Measures

The device you bring home — typically called a home sleep apnea test, or HSAT — is classified as a Type 3 sleep study. That classification comes from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, and it matters because it tells you exactly which data channels the device is recording.

A standard Type 3 home test monitors four things:

  • Airflow — via a nasal cannula, measuring whether air moves in and out of your airway
  • Respiratory effort — via chest and abdominal belts, detecting whether your rib cage and belly are attempting to expand
  • Oxygen saturation (SpO₂) — via a finger pulse oximeter
  • Heart rate — also from the oximeter

That is the complete list.

The device counts how many times per hour your airflow drops significantly (hypopneas) or stops entirely (apneas) and cross-references those events with oxygen desaturations. The result is your AHI — apnea-hypopnea index — which is the number used to stage the severity of obstructive sleep apnea: mild (5–15 events per hour), moderate (15–30), or severe (above 30).

These measurements are well-validated for one specific purpose: identifying obstructive sleep apnea in patients with a high pretest probability and no significant comorbidities. If you snore loudly, wake with a dry mouth, and your partner has witnessed you stop breathing, the home test was designed for someone like you.

What the Home Test Does Not Measure

Here is where the gap opens.

A Type 3 study does not include electroencephalography (EEG) — the brain-wave monitoring that tells a sleep technologist whether you are actually asleep or just lying still with your eyes closed. Without EEG, the device cannot distinguish between a true apnea and a breath-holding pause during wakefulness. It also cannot stage your sleep at all. That means the AHI the device reports is a total-events-over-total-recording-time number, not a total-events-over-total-sleep-time number, and those two denominators can differ substantially.

The home test also omits:

  • Leg movement sensors — so periodic limb movement disorder, which can fragment sleep and cause daytime fatigue that mimics apnea, goes completely undetected
  • Body positioning — sleep apnea is often worse on the back, and the device won't tell you whether your AHI was driven entirely by supine sleep
  • Snoring acoustics — the cannula detects airflow but not sound, so the test may miss upper airway resistance syndrome, a milder but real condition where the airway narrows without fully closing
  • Carbon dioxide monitoring — relevant for possible central sleep apnea or obesity hypoventilation syndrome

The practical consequence: an at-home study can return a negative result (AHI below 5) even when a patient has genuine sleep-disordered breathing. This is not a device failure. It is a scope-of-measurement failure. The test was not designed to catch everything, and it does not.

In-Lab Polysomnography: The Type 1 Standard

The in-lab polysomnogram (PSG) is a Type 1 study. It includes everything the home test measures plus EEG, chin and leg EMG, body position, snore microphone, and continuous video monitoring — all recorded under the direct supervision of a trained technologist.

The practical difference is not marginal. Studies comparing home and lab tests in the same patients have found that the lab study frequently reclassifies severity. A 2018 trial in the American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine reported that roughly 20% of patients with negative home tests had clinically significant sleep apnea on subsequent in-lab PSG.

The tradeoffs are real. A lab study costs more — typically $2,000 to $6,000 in the U.S., though insurance usually covers it after a referral — and it requires you to sleep in a hospital or dedicated sleep center with electrodes glued to your scalp and someone watching through a camera. That first-night effect (lower sleep quality in an unfamiliar environment) is well documented, though experienced technologists account for it when scoring the study.

The home test costs $150 to $500 out of pocket and you sleep in your own bed. The tradeoff is simple: convenience and lower cost versus diagnostic completeness.

Who This Is For

You are a good candidate for an at-home sleep study if:

  • You have loud, habitual snoring (witnessed by a partner)
  • You have observed apneas or choking/gasping episodes
  • Your BMI is above 30 and your neck circumference is above 17 inches (men) or 16 inches (women)
  • You have no significant heart, lung, or neuromuscular disease
  • You do not have symptoms strongly suggestive of central sleep apnea (for example, periods of shallow breathing that follow a distinct crescendo-decrescendo pattern called Cheyne-Stokes respiration)

If those criteria fit, a home study is the appropriate first step in most clinical guidelines, including those from the AASM and the European Respiratory Society.

You should push for an in-lab study if:

  • Your home test came back negative but your symptoms persist
  • You have congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, or a history of stroke
  • You take long-acting opioids or other respiratory suppressants
  • You have a history of central or mixed apneas
  • You cannot tolerate the home testing equipment (the nasal cannula or finger probe)

A Note on the False Negative

That negative result on a home study is not reassuring the way a negative lab result is. If you are symptomatic, a negative home test should not end the investigation. It should prompt a conversation with your sleep provider about whether a Type 1 study is warranted.

I keep a folder — literally a manila folder on my desk — of home sleep test reports that came back "negative" or "not significant" from patients who walked into my clinic describing textbook OSA. Every time I send one of those patients for an in-lab study, I check the box. So far, about one in three has come back positive for mild or moderate obstructive sleep apnea. That is a selection bias: these are patients whose pretest probability was higher because their symptoms were strong. But it is also a reminder that a test is only as good as the question it was built to answer.

The at-home sleep study answers one question well: Do you have moderate or severe obstructive sleep apnea? If the answer is yes, you have what you need to start treatment. If the answer is no, you have not ruled out the condition. You have only ruled out the version of it that the home test was designed to see.
