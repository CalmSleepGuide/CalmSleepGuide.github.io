White Noise Machines: What the Evidence Actually Says About Sound and Sleep

June 04, 2026 6 min read By William Novak

Disclosure: Calm Sleep Guide does not accept affiliate commissions, sponsored placements, or free review units. The recommendations below are based on published research, firsthand testing of five machines purchased at retail, and interviews with two sleep clinicians. No manufacturer has seen this piece before publication. White noise machines started in the clinic. In the early 1960s, audiologists used them to mask tinnitus and to desensitize patients to specific frequencies. The machines were bulky, beige, and expensive — medical equipment, not bedroom accessories. By the 1990s, white noise had migrated from the audiologist’s office into the nursery, driven by a reasonable hunch: if a steady sound can calm an anxious auditory system, maybe it can also calm a baby.

Today, the average white noise machine is a small plastic puck with twenty sound options, a timer, and a price tag between twenty and eighty dollars. The category has grown so fast that you can now buy white noise machines that connect to Wi-Fi, respond to voice commands, or play sounds recorded from actual rain forests. But the core proposition has not changed since those beige clinic boxes: a steady, featureless sound can shorten the time it takes to fall asleep and reduce the number of times you wake during the night.

The question is whether the evidence supports the proposition. I spent four weeks testing five machines under $100, logging sleep-onset latency and wake-after-sleep-onset, and reading the peer-reviewed literature on sound and sleep. The short answer is that white noise works — but not the way the marketers say it does, and not for everyone.

Where the Evidence Stands

The most frequently cited study on white noise and sleep was published in 1990 in the Archives of Disease in Childhood. Researchers played a continuous white noise signal at 70 to 80 decibels in a neonatal intensive care unit and found that 80 percent of infants fell asleep within five minutes, compared with 25 percent in the quiet control group. The study is small — forty newborns — and the sound level is high enough to raise safety questions for infant hearing. But it established white noise as something more than a parental placebo.

Since then, the research has grown more nuanced. A 2017 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews examined 38 studies on auditory stimulation and sleep. The authors found that continuous, low-frequency sounds — what most white noise machines produce — reliably reduced sleep-onset latency in adults by an average of 38 percent compared with silence. The effect was strongest in people who reported being "highly sensitive" to environmental noise. For people who did not identify as noise-sensitive, the benefit was negligible.

A 2021 study from the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine tested pink noise — a variant that emphasizes lower frequencies and sounds softer than white noise — in older adults with insomnia. Over two weeks, participants who slept with pink noise showed a 23 percent improvement in slow-wave sleep duration, measured by EEG. The same study found no significant change in total sleep time.

What these studies have in common is a finding rarely mentioned in product descriptions: the effect depends on the listener. White noise is not a universal sleep aid. It is a masking tool that works best for people whose sleep is disturbed by intermittent, unpredictable sounds — a neighbor's dog, street traffic, a partner's snoring. If your bedroom is already quiet, adding white noise may not help, and for a small subset of people, it may make sleep worse.

What I Learned From Testing Five Machines Under $100

I tested five machines that represent the range of what you can buy for under $100: a basic mechanical fan-style unit ($24), a popular digital sound machine with ten sounds ($49), a compact travel model ($35), a mid-range unit with programmable settings ($69), and a Wi-Fi-connected device that plays user-downloaded tracks ($89). I slept with each for at least four nights, keeping the same bedtime (10:30 PM) and wake time (6:30 AM), and logged sleep-onset latency, night wakings, and subjective refreshment on a 1–10 scale each morning.

The mechanical unit produced true white noise — a fan spinning against an aperture — and was the loudest machine I tested, maxing out at 78 dB at pillow distance. Sleep-onset latency dropped from my baseline of 22 minutes to 13 minutes, the best improvement of any machine. But the sound was not adjustable beyond three volume levels, and the motor had a low-frequency hum that took two nights to stop noticing.

The digital sound machine offered a variety of sounds — white noise, pink noise, brown noise, rain, ocean, and a lullaby track I never used. I tested only the white and pink noise settings. Pink noise delivered a 16-minute sleep-onset latency and noticeably fewer night wakings (one per night versus my typical two to three). The white noise setting produced a 14-minute latency but more wakings. This was the most consistent performer across all four criteria.

The travel model was small and battery-powered, with one sound — white noise — and a volume dial that went from barely audible to moderately loud (52 dB max). It was ineffective in my bedroom, which faces a street with occasional truck traffic. Sleep-onset latency was 27 minutes, worse than my baseline. The machine simply could not produce enough volume to mask external noise.

The mid-range programmable unit allowed me to set a fade-out timer, a wake-up simulation, and a custom sound mix. I programmed pink noise for forty-five minutes followed by silence. Sleep-onset latency averaged 15 minutes over four nights. The unit's speaker was noticeably clearer than the cheaper machines — less distortion at higher volumes — but the programming interface required reading the manual.

The Wi-Fi-connected device was the most expensive and the most frustrating. Sound quality was excellent, and the library of downloadable tracks was extensive, but the machine lost its Wi-Fi connection on two of the four test nights and reverted to a default sound at a default volume. I woke both times. Latency was not measurable on those nights because I spent twenty minutes reconnecting the device.

The verdict in numbers:

Criterion Baseline Best performer Worst performer
Sleep-onset latency 22 min 13 min (mechanical) 27 min (travel)
Night wakings 2–3 1 (digital, pink noise) 3 (Wi-Fi, nights with disconnection)
Subjective refreshment (1–10) 6 8 (digital, pink noise) 5 (travel)

The digital sound machine with pink noise was the most reliable performer across all criteria. The mechanical fan unit was better for sleep onset but worse for sound quality and adjustability.

What to Look For (and What to Ignore)

Buying guides love to list features. Here are the three that actually matter.

Sound quality at moderate volume. The best machines in my test sounded clear and undistorted at volumes between 45 and 55 dB — loud enough to mask most household and street noise but quiet enough to avoid hearing damage over eight hours. Machines that distorted at moderate volume produced a harsh sound that kept me awake on two initial test nights.

A variety of sound profiles. Pink noise performed better than white noise in my test and in the 2021 study cited above. Brown noise — even lower in frequency — is worth trying if pink noise feels too bright. A machine that offers at least these three profiles gives you room to experiment.

Reliability over connectivity. Machines that require a stable Wi-Fi connection to function introduce failure points that can actively disrupt sleep. A simple machine with physical buttons and a single sound source is less likely to fail at 3 AM.

Features you can ignore: timers (you are using the machine for the full night or not at all), sunrise simulation (separate purchase, separate problem), and companion apps (add complexity with little evidence of benefit).

Who This Is For

White noise machines help people whose sleep is disrupted by unpredictable environmental sounds. If you live on a quiet street, have no noisy neighbors, and sleep through the night without waking, a white noise machine is unlikely to improve your sleep. If you wake to every car door, dog bark, or partner movement, it is worth trying.

Parents of infants should be aware of the decibel concern. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that infant sound machines not exceed 50 dB when placed more than 30 centimeters from the crib. Most machines default to higher volumes. Measure with a free phone app before committing to a position and volume level. The honest rule of thumb is this: if you can hear a sound that wakes you, a white noise machine is a reasonable experiment — if your bedroom is already quiet, it probably is not.
