Yoga for Sleep: What the Evidence Actually Says

June 05, 2026 7 min read By Andrew Pemberton

Walk into any yoga studio and you will hear it: a few more minutes in Savasana will fix your insomnia. Roll out a mat before bed, hold a few gentle stretches, and sleep will arrive like a reflex.

That version of the story is comfortable. It is also incomplete.

Yoga for sleep has been studied more seriously than most wellness trends, and the data is genuinely interesting. But what the evidence actually supports is narrower — and more useful — than what the marketing suggests. Here is what we know, what we do not, and what to do with the gap.

What Most People Do

The typical approach to using yoga for sleep is borrowed from the end of any public class: floor work, dim lights, a long Savasana. People replicate this at home — fifteen minutes of forward folds, legs up the wall, maybe a recorded nidra script — expecting that relaxing the body will automatically relax the brain.

For some, it does. For many, it does not.

The assumption is that yoga works primarily as a muscle-relaxant — stretch the hamstrings, quiet the nervous system. That is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Yoga also demands attention. It requires you to track your breath, hold a shape, feel a sensation. For a racing mind, that focus can be a relief. For a mind that is already quiet but the body won't settle — restless legs, hormonal flushing, pregnancy discomfort — the attentional demand can feel like one more thing to manage.

Most people also practice too close to bed, or too hard. A vigorous vinyasa at 9 PM raises heart rate and core temperature, both of which oppose sleep onset. The common instruction to "do what feels good" ignores the fact that what feels good in the moment — a deep backbend, a strong flow — may leave you wired rather than wind-downed.

What the Evidence Suggests

The research on yoga for sleep is better than the research on most non-pharmacological sleep interventions, but that is a low bar. Here is what the controlled trials actually show, organized by mechanism and population.

Mechanisms: Three pathways, not one

Well-established: Yoga reduces perceived stress. This is the strongest finding. In a 2020 meta-analysis by Wu et al. in Frontiers in Psychiatry covering 19 randomized trials, yoga significantly reduced cortisol levels and self-reported stress compared to no intervention. Since stress is a primary driver of insomnia in many people, this alone predicts a sleep benefit — but indirectly. Yoga reduces stress; better stress management makes sleep easier. That is not the same as yoga causing sleep.

Plausible but thin: Yoga increases parasympathetic tone. The deep breathing and slow movement in restorative or yin styles activate the vagus nerve, shifting the autonomic nervous system toward rest-and-digest. A 2017 study by Tyagi and Cohen in Medical Hypotheses laid out the mechanism clearly: slow, rhythmic breathing at six breaths per minute can entrain heart rate variability and improve baroreflex sensitivity. The logic is sound. But most yoga-for-sleep studies combine poses, breathing, and meditation, making it impossible to isolate which component does the work.

Folk wisdom: Yoga boosts melatonin. I have seen this claimed frequently. I have not found a controlled human trial that supports it. The common citation is a small 2013 study of 30 women with premenstrual symptoms who did 35 minutes of yoga daily for 12 weeks and reported better sleep quality — but melatonin was never measured. The claim appears to be an extrapolation, not a finding.

What the trials measured, by population

People with insomnia. This is where the evidence is strongest. A 2014 randomized trial by Khalsa et al. in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine assigned 30 adults with chronic insomnia to an 8-week yoga program or a self-care control. The yoga group showed significant improvements in sleep efficiency (the percentage of time in bed actually asleep) and total sleep time, with effect sizes comparable to what you would expect from cognitive behavioral therapy — about 30 to 40 minutes more sleep per night on average. The program was gentle: restorative poses, breathwork, meditation. No vigorous flow.

Menopausal women. Hormonal fluctuation during perimenopause and menopause disrupts sleep through hot flashes, night sweats, and changes in melatonin production. A 2020 trial in Menopause by Afonso et al. randomized 60 women with moderate-to-severe hot flashes to 16 weeks of yoga or a waitlist. The yoga group reported fewer hot flash-related sleep disturbances and better overall sleep quality on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. The benefit appeared to come from reduced hot flash intensity, not from direct sleep regulation.

Pregnant women. The data here is mixed but leans positive. A 2015 Cochrane review by Liddle and Pennick found that yoga during pregnancy reduced nighttime awakenings and improved subjective sleep quality compared to standard prenatal care. The effect was small — roughly a one-point improvement on a twenty-one-point sleep scale — and studies varied wildly in style and duration. The signal is real but not dramatic.

Older adults. A 2018 systematic review in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society (Sivaramakrishnan et al.) pooled data from 12 trials and found that yoga improved both subjective sleep quality and sleep efficiency in adults over 60. The improvements were modest — about 7% improvement in sleep efficiency, which translates to roughly 30 more minutes of actual sleep in an eight-hour night. Importantly, the trials that used seated or chair-based yoga showed similar benefits to those using floor poses, suggesting the mechanism is not physical exertion but nervous-system regulation.

Restless legs syndrome (RLS). A single small but well-designed trial in 2013 (Innes et al., Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine) gave 13 women with RLS an 8-week yoga program. RLS severity scores dropped by an average of 45%, and sleep quality improved significantly. Thirteen people is too small to generalize. But the mechanism is plausible: yoga reduces sympathetic activation, and RLS symptoms are known to worsen under stress. Replication is needed, but this is a promising lead.

Parents of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). No controlled trials specifically examine this population for yoga and sleep. The closest relevant data comes from caregiver stress research: a 2019 pilot study of mindfulness-based interventions for parents of children with ASD showed improvements in sleep quality, and yoga is often included as a component. But the evidence is indirect.

How often, what kind, and when

The trials that work share a profile: gentle or restorative styles, practiced 20 to 45 minutes per session, three to five times per week, with at least one hour between practice and bedtime. The styles that show benefit are not power yoga or hot yoga. They are slow, floor-based, breath-heavy: yin, restorative, gentle hatha, yoga nidra.

Hatha yoga — the slow, posture-holding style most common in studios — shows the most consistent results across populations, likely because it pairs physical activity with deliberate breath regulation. Yoga nidra, which is essentially a guided-body-scan meditation done lying down, shows similar benefits in the insomnia trials, sometimes with even shorter practice times (20 minutes).

What I Actually Do

I do not have a morning practice. I do not wake up at 5 AM for sun salutations. If you do, that is fine. But the evidence suggests that for sleep, evening practice is where the leverage is — and that most people overestimate how much movement they need.

Here is an honest rule of thumb, grounded in the data above:

Do ten minutes of a single slow thing, at least an hour before bed, on the floor, in the dark or near-dark, without music.

Not a sequence. One thing. Legs up the wall, a supported child's pose, a reclined twist, or simply lying on your back with your hands on your ribs and counting your breaths to six on the inhale and six on the exhale. The research that works uses multiple components — poses, breathing, meditation — but no trial suggests you need all three to benefit. The common denominator is slower breathing and a shift in attention away from thought and toward sensation.

If you have restless legs, try a gentle supine twist or a forward fold before the legs-up-the-wall position. If you are pregnant, side-lying poses with a bolster under the top knee replicate the safest sleep position and may reduce nighttime discomfort. If you are menopausal and dealing with night sweats, keep the room cool (18–20°C, which is the evidence-based temperature range for sleep) and practice in light clothing — yoga does not need to generate heat to work.

If ten minutes is too much, do five. If five is too much, lie on the floor for three minutes and breathe. The dose-response curve seems to flatten early — meaning the first few minutes do most of the work.

What This Piece Did Not Answer

This piece did not tell you exactly which poses to do, because the evidence does not support a single sequence. It did not tell you to replace your sleep medication with yoga, because no responsible trial has tested that directly. It did not tell you that yoga cures insomnia, because it does not — but it may help a subset of people, particularly those whose sleep problems are tied to stress, mild anxiety, or autonomic over-arousal.

What the evidence actually suggests is narrower than the claims, but more honest. Yoga might not fix your sleep. It might give you a few more good nights a month than you would otherwise have. For something that costs only a mat and ten minutes, that is not nothing.

If you want to go deeper: look for the original Khalsa trial on Pubmed, search for Wu et al. 2020 on cortisol and yoga, and pay attention to whether a given study measured objective sleep (actigraphy or polysomnography) or subjective sleep (questionnaires). The two do not always agree, and the gap between them is where the real story lives.
